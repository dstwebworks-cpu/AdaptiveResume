A forklift operator resume is read for two things: which trucks you can run, and what your numbers were when you ran them. A sit-down counterbalance, a stand-up reach truck, and an order picker are different machines with different learning curves, and a posting that says "reach truck experience required" is a filter. A resume that just says "forklift" doesn't pass it.

The second thing — and the one this guide spends real time on — is how you word your training. Template resumes everywhere say "forklift certified." There is no national forklift license or certification, and the reader who matters most, the safety-conscious hiring manager, knows it. Getting that one line right does more for you than any formatting trick.

## Lead with your trucks and your numbers

Under each employer, give the context in one line before the bullets — shift, the trucks you ran, what you moved, and the numbers you were held to.

**Before:** "Operated forklift to move products around the warehouse."

**After:** "Ran a stand-up reach truck and order picker on second shift, trained and evaluated under OSHA 1910.178; picked ~[N] lines/hour at [N]% scanner accuracy in [SAP EWM / Manhattan]; [N] months incident-free."

The second version answers the questions the first one forces a reader to guess at: which truck, how fast, how accurate, how safe. It also names the warehouse system by brand, which matters more than it looks — postings filter on it.

## Name the truck, not just "forklift"

OSHA's powered industrial truck standard, OSHA groups powered industrial trucks under its standard, 29 CFR 1910.178, into seven classes, Class I through Class VII — from electric riders to rough-terrain machines. You don't need to recite the list, but you do need to name what you've operated in the words hiring managers use:

- **Sit-down counterbalance** (Class I electric, or Class IV/V internal combustion) — the classic dock-and-aisle forklift.
- **Stand-up reach truck** (Class II narrow aisle) — high racking, tight aisles.
- **Order picker** (Class II) — you go up with the load, picking at height.
- **Electric pallet jack / walkie rider** (Class III) — the workhorse of pick lines and trailers.

For each type, a year count and a setting ("reach truck, [N] years, racking to [N] feet") is worth more than an adjective. If you've run internal-combustion trucks outdoors, or a tow tractor, or rough-terrain equipment on a yard, say so — those are separate skills.

One more line that separates operators who know their machine from operators who drive it: the **data plate**. Every truck's rated capacity assumes a load center — commonly rated at 24 inches, the distance from the fork face to the load's center of gravity — and capacity drops as the load center moves out. If you've handled long, wide, or off-center loads and adjusted for it, that's a real bullet, and it tells a safety manager you understand why the plate matters.

## "Forklift certified" — what's actually true, and why the honest version reads better

Here is the wording that holds up. OSHA's powered industrial truck standard, 29 CFR 1910.178, requires employers to train and evaluate every operator, to re-evaluate at least once every three years, and to certify in writing that it was done. There is no national forklift license, so the honest wording is employer training:

> Forklift operator — trained and evaluated under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.178 (sit-down counterbalance, stand-up reach, order picker, pallet jack); last evaluation [year].

Why bother, when every template says "certified"? Because the person reading your resume for a warehouse job has sat through the same standard you have. "Certified" with no issuer named reads like a phrase copied off a template. "Trained and evaluated under OSHA 1910.178" reads like an operator who knows exactly what their employer signed. When the job is keeping people from getting hurt around several thousand pounds of moving steel, sounding precise about safety is the whole audition.

Two more safety lines worth writing precisely:

- **Pre-shift inspections.** The standard, at 1910.178(q)(7), requires trucks to be examined before being placed in service — at least daily, and after each shift where trucks run round the clock. "[N] years of pre-shift inspections, defects tagged and reported same day" is a line a safety manager stops on.
- **Hazmat awareness.** If your building shipped hazardous materials and you took DOT (Department of Transportation) hazmat employee training under 49 CFR 172.704, list it with the year. Only list it if you actually took it.

If your site tracked a Total Recordable Incident Rate (TRIR — recordable injuries per 200,000 hours worked) and you know where your shift landed, you can write it; if you don't know the figure, "[N] months / [N] years incident-free" is just as readable and easier to stand behind.

## RF scanning and the warehouse system

If your building ran RF (radio frequency) scanning — the handheld or truck-mounted scanner that drives your picks and putaways — say so, and name the system behind it by its actual product name if you know it: SAP Extended Warehouse Management (SAP EWM), Manhattan, Blue Yonder, or whatever your building ran. "Used a WMS" (warehouse management system) without the brand doesn't pass the filter that "Manhattan experience preferred" creates.

Environment counts too. Cold storage and freezer work is its own qualification — the pace, the gear, the condensation on the truck — so if you've picked or hauled in a [freezer / cooler] environment, write it with the hours: "[N] hours/shift in freezer conditions." Trailer loading and unloading, dock work, wrapping and staging, cycle counting from the truck — all of it belongs on the page in plain words.

## Warehouse worker resume — same rules, whatever you're pushing

If your title is warehouse associate, picker, packer, loader, or material handler, everything above applies at your scale — the truck section just gets smaller and the numbers get bigger. Replace "responsible for picking and packing orders" with what you actually moved and how well: "picked ~[N] lines/hour at [N]% accuracy on an RF scanner; loaded [N] trailers/shift; [N] months incident-free." Name the equipment you do run, even if it's a manual pallet jack, and name the system.

Then write the lines that point upward: trained new hires on the pick line, ran the cycle count team, covered the lead's duties, learned a second truck type. ## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** If you've run trucks in three buildings, or you came to the floor from another trade, that history is your advantage — don't cram it into one page to satisfy a convention.

The honest rule: **two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive role to describe.** Every line still has to earn its space. Cut duties every operator shares — "followed all safety procedures" — and keep truck types, numbers, systems, and your record.

One genuine exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change. Federal warehouse and materials-handling jobs exist, so if that's where you're headed, treat it as a hard requirement.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, a one-line scope statement under every employer, and a clearly labeled block for equipment and systems. Skip the graphics and skill bars — the software that reads your file first handles plain text best.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Operated [sit-down counterbalance / stand-up reach / order picker / pallet jack] on [shift]; trained and evaluated under OSHA 1910.178, last evaluation [year]"
- "Picked ~[N] lines/hour at [N]% scanner accuracy in [SAP EWM / Manhattan / Blue Yonder]"
- "Moved ~[N] pallets/shift across [receiving / putaway / replenishment / shipping]"
- "Loaded and unloaded [N] trailers/day; staged, wrapped, and shipped at [N]% on-time"
- "[N] months incident-free; completed pre-shift inspections daily and tagged [N] defects for repair"
- "Worked [N] hours/shift in [freezer / cold storage] conditions at [temperature] without missing rate"
- "Completed DOT hazmat employee training (49 CFR 172.704), [year]; handled [hazard class] freight"
- "Trained [N] new operators on [truck type] and the [WMS] scanner flow"
