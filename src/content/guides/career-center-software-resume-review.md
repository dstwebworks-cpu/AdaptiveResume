A college career center buys resume-review software for a good reason: a small staff cannot review thousands of resumes a semester, and students want feedback at midnight before an application closes. The tools that exist do part of that job well. They score a resume against a rubric, flag formatting problems, and give the student something to fix before an appointment.

What they do not do is find what the student left off. A junior who spent two summers in a research lab writes "assisted with research," and a scorer will grade that sentence for verbs and length without ever asking which techniques, which instruments, or which dataset. The gap between what students did and what their resumes say is the biggest problem a career center has, and it is the one the software category was not built to solve.

## What a resume-review tool should do for the student

Hold any tool, including ours, to five requirements.

**1. It should start from what the student did, not from what they wrote.** A student's real history is in their jobs, internships, campus roles, labs, and coursework projects. A tool that only grades the existing text cannot find the missing lines. A tool that works backwards from the roles can.

**2. Every added line should be confirmed by the student.** A career office's name is on the advice its tools give. Nothing should land on a resume the student did not say yes to, and the tool should hand over a record of what was proposed and what was confirmed, so a career coach can read it.

**3. Credentials should be asked about, never assumed.** Certifications, licenses, and language proficiencies are checkable, and a resume that claims one the student does not hold is worse than one that leaves it off. A tool that adds a credential because it fits the major has created a problem for the student and for the office.

**4. The finished file should read cleanly to screening software.** For a student, that means one column, the standard headings (Education, Experience, Skills), and no graphics or photos. Students love templates, and applicant tracking systems do not.

**5. It should be readable by the student who most needs it.** First-generation students and students whose first language is not English are often the ones a scoring rubric serves least well, because the rubric assumes they know what a strong resume looks like. Plain questions ("Did you use SPSS or R in that lab?") serve them better than a score.

## How per-student pricing distorts the buy

Most institutional tools are sold as an annual campus license sized to enrollment, paid whether or not a given student logs in, and the price is rarely published. Where public records show it, the amounts range from about $27,000 a year at a college of about 15,000 students to about $116,000 for one year at a large multi-campus university. The pricing is simple to budget, and it hides the number that matters: the price per student who actually used the tool. Take an illustration, not a real campus: a campus of 20,000 students paying a flat license, where 3,000 students log in and 800 finish a resume, is paying the whole license for 800 resumes, which is many times the per-enrolled-student figure the vendor quoted.

Ask any vendor for two numbers: students who logged in and students who completed a resume, per year, at a campus like yours. Then divide the license fee by the second number. That is the price of the product.

## What a rebuilt student resume looks like

This is a sample student, not a real person.

**Before:** "Research assistant, Department of Biology. Assisted with research and lab duties."

**After:** "Undergraduate research assistant, Department of Biology, 2 years: ran PCR and gel electrophoresis on about 200 samples for a plant-genetics study, maintained the lab's sample inventory in Benchling, presented a poster at the university's undergraduate research symposium, and trained 3 new lab assistants on sterile technique."

In a real rebuild, every item in the second version would be true: the techniques, the sample count, the system, the poster, and the training would all be things the student did and never wrote down, because "assisted with research" was the phrase the student thought a resume required. No rubric would have found any of it.

## How to run a one-semester pilot

A career center does not need a campus-wide license to find out whether a resume tool works for its students. A one-semester pilot with a defined group answers the question at a fraction of the cost and gives the office real numbers for the budget conversation.

**Pick the group.** One college within the university, one graduating class in a program with a strong job market, or the students who book appointments in a given month. A group of roughly 100 to 300 students is large enough to learn from and small enough to support.

**Decide the two numbers in advance.** Students who completed a resume, and students who reported using it in an application. Everything else, including logins and satisfaction scores, is secondary.

**Keep the staff in the loop.** The value of a tool that records what was proposed and what the student confirmed is that a coach can read the per-student record before an appointment and spend the meeting on strategy instead of on the resume's first draft. Pilot that workflow with two or three coaches, and ask them whether appointments got shorter or better.

**Compare on cost per completed resume.** At the end of the semester, divide what the pilot cost by the number of completed resumes, and do the same arithmetic for the tool you already license, using its own completion number. That comparison is the budget case, in either direction.

## Accessibility and the students who need it most

Any tool a career center adopts should work on a phone, because that is where many students will use it, and it should work for a student using a screen reader. Ask the vendor how it tests with a screen reader, and try the tool yourself on a phone at 360 pixels wide. A resume tool that only works on a laptop in the career center has quietly excluded the commuter student who most needed it.

## Questions to ask any vendor

1. Does the tool find lines the student left off, or grade the lines already there?
2. Does the student confirm every added line, and can a coach see that record?
3. What happens to a certification the student never mentioned?
4. What is the price per student who completed a resume, not per student enrolled?
5. Does the finished file read cleanly to applicant tracking systems, or is it a template?

## Format notes for student resumes

One page is usually right for a student with one or two roles, and two pages is normal once a student has more than one substantive job or a research history. Keep it reverse chronological, one column, no photos or graphics, and put Education first while the degree is the strongest line. Students applying to federal internships and jobs through USAJOBS should know that federal applications cap at two pages as of the September 2025 OPM change. [O*NET's occupation profiles](https://www.onetonline.org/) give students the task and skill vocabulary employers use for each role, which is a better starting point than a template.
