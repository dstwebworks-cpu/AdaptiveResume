A work readiness assessment answers one question: can this person do a set of measured things, today, at a scored level? That is a useful question, and most workforce programs have to answer it at intake. It is not the question an employer asks. An employer asks what the person has done, and a score does not say.

This guide is for the people who run intake. It covers what the common readiness instruments measure, what the WIOA regulations actually require of an assessment, where the assessment stops and the employer's question begins, and what a program's record should hold so that a participant leaves with more than a score.

## What the common instruments measure

Programs use several instruments, and they measure different things.

- **ACT WorkKeys** and its National Career Readiness Certificate score applied skills: applied math, graphic literacy, and workplace documents. Employers who ask for the certificate are asking for a level on those three.
- **CASAS** measures adult basic skills in reading, math, and listening, and is common in adult education and English-language programs.
- **TABE** is an academic test of reading, math, and language, used to place participants and to document measurable skill gains.
- **Job readiness or soft-skills inventories**, often locally built questionnaires, ask about attendance, communication, following instructions, and workplace behavior. Youth programs use a youth version of the same idea.

Each of these produces a level or a score. None of them produces a sentence an employer can read about what the participant did on a job, in a facility, as a volunteer, or in a home.

## What WIOA actually requires

The Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) requires an assessment; it does not name the instrument.

For youth, the statute requires an objective assessment of each participant's academic levels, skill levels, and service needs, including a review of basic skills, occupational skills, prior work experience, employability, interests, aptitudes, supportive service needs, and developmental needs. That list is in WIOA section 129(c)(1)(A), codified at 29 U.S.C. 3164(c)(1)(A), and the youth program regulations carry it at 20 CFR 681.420(a)(1). Prior work experience is on it, which means the assessment is supposed to capture what the young person has already done, not only what they score.

For adults and dislocated workers, comprehensive and specialized assessments of skill levels and service needs are an individualized career service under 20 CFR 678.430(b), one that must be made available when the one-stop determines it is appropriate for the person. The same regulation makes the individual employment plan a career service, and a plan is built on what the person can do and has done.

So the regulations already ask for two things the score alone does not give: prior experience, and a plan built on it. Programs meet that requirement today through case-manager interviews and notes. The quality of the notes depends on the case manager's caseload.

## Where the assessment stops

A score is a point on a scale. It tells you the participant reads workplace documents at a certain level. It does not tell you that she ran the front counter of a family restaurant for six years, handled the cash drawer, opened and closed, trained two cousins, and dealt with every unhappy customer on a Friday night. Those facts are what get her the front-desk job at the clinic, and they exist only if someone asks and writes them down in a form an employer will read.

The gap is widest for the participants programs care most about:

- **Dislocated workers with long tenure**, whose skills became invisible to them years ago.
- **Justice-involved participants**, whose facility work and pre-incarceration work are real and go unrecorded because nobody asks how to state them.
- **Youth**, whose experience is informal, part-time, seasonal, or unpaid, and who have never been shown that it counts.
- **Caregivers returning to work**, whose gap is a fact and whose skills did not stop.

A readiness score for these participants is often fine. The record is empty.

## What the record should hold

A participant's file, and the resume they leave with, should hold three things next to the assessment score.

**1. A confirmed inventory of what they have done.** Every job, formal or informal, stated as what it was, with the equipment, systems, standards, and people involved. Facility work is stated as what it was. A gap gets one plain dated line.

**2. Credentials that were asked about, never assumed.** A forklift certification, a food handler card, a CDL, a state license, a training completion. These are checkable, and a record that claims one the participant does not hold does harm.

**3. A resume that screening software reads.** Single column, standard headings, no graphics, because the first employer's applicant tracking system is the first reader.

Here is the difference for one participant. This is a sample, not a real person.

**Before, from the intake form:** "Restaurant. 6 years. Counter."

**After, confirmed line by line:** "Front-counter lead at a 40-seat family restaurant for six years: opened and closed the store, balanced a daily cash drawer, took phone and walk-in orders on a Toast POS, trained two new counter staff, and handled customer complaints directly on the busiest shifts."

Her readiness score did not change. Her file did.
