A pilot resume is the most structured document in hiring: every reader — airline recruiter, charter chief pilot, insurance underwriter — scans for the same four things in the same order. Certificates. Ratings. Hours. Currency. A beautiful resume that buries those is worse than a plain one that leads with them.

## The block that goes at the top

Right under your name and contact line, before any work history:

- **Certificates** — exactly as the FAA words them: Commercial Pilot, Airline Transport Pilot, Certified Flight Instructor (CFI/CFII/MEI). Certificate number optional; some operators want it, so have it ready.
- **Ratings** — instrument, multi-engine, and any type ratings by their real designators.
- **Medical** — class and date. A current first-class medical is a screening item; say so plainly.
- **Currency** — flight review date, IPC if relevant, night/IFR currency as applicable.

This block is pure fact — which makes it the easiest section to get exactly right and the most damaging place to round up. Aviation is the one industry where your resume gets checked against logbooks and FAA records as a matter of routine. Every number must match what your logbook can show.

## The hours table

Standard practice, and readers expect the format: a small table of total time, PIC, SIC, cross-country, night, instrument (actual and simulated), and multi-engine, plus time in type for anything relevant to the job. Round DOWN to the logbook, never up. If you're between logbook audits, an honest "1,240+ (as of 07/2026)" beats a suspiciously round 1,300.

**Before:** "Experienced commercial pilot with extensive flight time in various aircraft."
**After:** a five-line hours table the reader absorbs in three seconds. In aviation, the table *is* the persuasion.

## Work history: operations, not adjectives

For each flying job: operator, aircraft flown, operation type (Part 91/135/121, if applicable), and what the flying actually was — freight at night, medevac on call, instruction toward checkrides. Non-flying jobs stay on the page if they're recent or explain a gap; one line each is enough.

## CFI, low-time, and career-change pilots

If you're building hours, your instructing *is* your experience: students soloed, checkride pass rate if you track it honestly, aircraft used. Career changers with a fresh commercial certificate: lead with the certificate block like everyone else, then let your prior career carry the professional-reliability weight — years of showing up, safety records, operational work. The certificate says you can fly; the history says you're employable.

## A&P mechanics: same logic, different block

Airframe & Powerplant certificate up top, IA if held, then aircraft types and inspection work. The certificate-first structure is identical — aviation hiring reads credentials before prose in every seat of the hangar.

## Format notes

One page is the strong convention for pilot resumes short of a long corporate career. Single column, no graphics — several operators run resumes through parsing software before a human sees them, and the hours table should be a simple table or aligned text, nothing decorative.
