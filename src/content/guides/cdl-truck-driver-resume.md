Truck driver resumes are screened by license line before anything else. A recruiter, or the software in front of them, looks for the class, the endorsements, and the years, and only then reads what you hauled and how safely. A resume that buries the license in a skills list at the bottom, or writes it vaguely ("CDL holder"), loses the reader in the first four seconds.

The second thing they read is the record: miles, freight, equipment, and safety. Most driver resumes say "delivered freight safely and on time" and never say how many miles, what kind of trailer, or how many roadside inspections came back clean. Those numbers are what a fleet safety manager is trying to find, and they are the numbers you already know.

## Lead with the license line, written exactly

Put the license line in the top third of the page, above the work history, and write it the way the DMV and the FMCSA write it.

**Before:** "CDL holder with tanker and hazmat, clean record, DOT certified."

**After:** "CDL Class A, Georgia, expires [month/year]. Endorsements: H (hazardous materials), N (tank), T (doubles/triples). DOT medical certificate current through [month/year]. FMCSA Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse registered. Entry-Level Driver Training completed [year], [provider]."

![Before and after example of an improved resume line - every line confirmed by the person](/img/guides/examples/cdl-truck-driver-resume-example.png)

The second version answers every screening question at once and matches the vocabulary in the posting. If you hold the X endorsement (tank and hazmat combined), write X rather than N and H separately. Write only what your license shows; a hazmat endorsement requires a Transportation Security Administration threat assessment and is one of the first things a carrier verifies.

## The parts of the job that carry the most weight

**Miles and years, stated plainly.** Total miles or average miles per year, years of Class A driving, and how much of it was over-the-road, regional, or local. "About 120,000 miles a year, over-the-road, 48 states" says more than any adjective.

**Freight and equipment.** Dry van, reefer, flatbed, tanker, doubles, intermodal, LTL. For flatbed, name load securement under the federal cargo securement rules (49 CFR Part 393) and the load types: steel, lumber, machinery, oversize with permits. For reefer, name temperature-controlled loads and the reefer units you ran. For tanker, name the product class and whether it was food grade or hazmat.

**Safety record, in checkable numbers.** Accident-free miles, years without a preventable accident, roadside inspections passed, and Compliance, Safety, Accountability (CSA) history if it helps you. Carriers pull your motor vehicle record and your Pre-Employment Screening Program report, so the resume should agree with both.

**Hours of service and logs.** Electronic logging device experience by system (Samsara, Motive, Omnitracs, or whatever your fleet ran), hours-of-service compliance under 49 CFR Part 395, and pre-trip and post-trip inspection discipline. A driver who can say "zero hours-of-service violations across [N] years on ELDs" is answering the question every safety manager asks.

**Customer and dock work.** On-time delivery rate, drop-and-hook versus live unload, lumper coordination, and any customer-facing accounts. Local and dedicated postings care about this more than over-the-road postings do.

## Endorsements, permits, and cards, as held

List every endorsement by its letter and name: H (hazardous materials), N (tank vehicles), X (tank and hazmat combined), T (doubles and triples), P (passenger), S (school bus). List restrictions honestly if you have one that matters, such as an automatic-transmission restriction, because the carrier will see it on the license anyway.

Add the cards and registrations a carrier checks: the DOT medical certificate with its expiration, the FMCSA Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse registration, a Transportation Worker Identification Credential (TWIC) if you run ports, and any state permits for oversize or overweight loads you have hauled under. If you completed Entry-Level Driver Training, list the provider and the date; if you were licensed before February 2022, it does not apply, and claiming it is the kind of detail a verification catches.

Defensive driving and company safety programs (for example, the Smith System) are worth a line each, with the year. So is a forklift certification if you loaded your own freight at customer sites.

## Owner-operators and specialized freight

If you have run under your own authority, write the business facts as facts: years operating, equipment owned, lanes and freight booked, revenue per mile if you know it, and the compliance you managed yourself (registration, inspections, IFTA fuel tax reporting, insurance). A carrier hiring you as a company driver reads that as a driver who understands the cost of every mile.

Specialized freight earns its own lines: oversize and overweight with escort coordination, auto transport, livestock, tanker with hazmat, or team driving. Name the freight, the equipment, and the permit type.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** A driver with twelve years across three carriers and two freight types has more that matters than one page holds. **Two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive job to describe.** What matters is that every line earns its space: miles, freight, equipment, safety numbers, and endorsements stay; duties every driver shares go.

Reverse chronological, single column, the license line above the work history, and no graphics or photos. Many carriers run applications through screening software before a recruiter sees them, and a two-column template or a photo can scramble the read. One exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change, and federal motor-vehicle-operator postings ask for details a carrier application does not.

Gaps get one plain dated line. A carrier will see the gap on the DOT employment history anyway (the regulations require them to verify the previous three years of driving employment), so the resume should say what the DOT history will say.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "CDL Class A, [state], endorsements [H/N/X/T]; DOT medical certificate current through [month/year]"
- "[N] years over-the-road, about [N] miles per year, [N] states; [N] accident-free miles"
- "Hauled [dry van/reefer/flatbed/tanker] freight: [load types]; secured flatbed loads under 49 CFR Part 393"
- "[N] roadside inspections with no out-of-service orders; zero hours-of-service violations on [ELD system]"
- "On-time delivery rate of [N]% across [N] loads per week on a [dedicated/regional] account"
- "Completed Entry-Level Driver Training, [provider], [year]; hazmat endorsement with TSA threat assessment [year]"
- "Owner-operator [years]: [N] trucks, [lanes], IFTA and DOT compliance managed in-house"
- "Trained [N] new drivers on pre-trip inspection and load securement"

## What screening software looks for on a truck driver resume

Filters commonly read for: CDL Class A, Class B, hazmat, tanker, doubles/triples, TWIC, DOT medical card, ELD, hours of service, pre-trip inspection, over-the-road, regional, local, dedicated, flatbed, reefer, dry van, LTL, drop and hook, load securement, defensive driving, clean MVR, and the specific ELD and dispatch systems the carrier runs. [O*NET's profile for heavy and tractor-trailer truck drivers](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/53-3032.00) has the full task list.

The highest-risk lines are the ones a carrier verifies in minutes: an endorsement you do not hold, a medical card that has lapsed, training you did not complete, or a safety record that disagrees with your motor vehicle record. Write what the license and the record show.
