A warehouse worker resume is read by a hiring manager or a staffing recruiter who fills shifts for a living. They are looking for three numbers and a short list: how fast you work, how accurate you are, how reliably you show up, and which equipment and systems you can run on day one. A resume that says "hard-working team player responsible for picking and packing orders" gives them none of it, and it looks like many other applications in the pile.

The good news is that warehouse work is measured closely, and in many buildings your rate, your accuracy, and your attendance were tracked every shift. Put those numbers on the page.

## Lead with your rate, your accuracy, and your equipment

**Before:** "Warehouse associate responsible for picking, packing, and shipping orders in a fast-paced environment."

**After:** "Warehouse associate at a [regional fulfillment center], [night] shift: picked about [140] units an hour on an RF scanner at [99.8]% accuracy; trained on the electric pallet jack and the pick-to-light line; [12] months with no safety incidents and no missed shifts."

The second version tells the manager the setting, the speed, the accuracy, the equipment, and the reliability in two lines. The numbers in a line like this usually come from what your warehouse already tracks.

## The numbers a warehouse manager reads

**Rate.** Give your rate in units per hour, lines per hour, cases per hour, or orders per shift, depending on how your building counted it. Write the unit your warehouse used, because "140 units an hour" and "140 lines an hour" are very different jobs. If you met or beat the standard, say so: "about 110% of the standard rate across peak season."

**Accuracy.** Give your pick accuracy, pack accuracy, or the error rate on your counts. Accuracy matters as much as speed, because a wrong item can cost the warehouse a return and a customer.

**Attendance and reliability.** Count your months without a missed shift, the overtime and peak-season shifts you worked, and any attendance or performance recognition. In a business that runs on full shifts, this is a selling point, not a filler line.

**Safety.** Write your months or years without a recordable incident, the safety training you completed, and any safety-team role.

## Where your numbers come from

Many warehouses show your rate on the scanner or a daily screen, and some states now require it in writing. California's warehouse quota law, for example, requires covered employers to give each worker [a written description of each quota](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=LAB&sectionNum=2101) they are subject to, including the number of tasks or items expected in a set time. New York, Washington, Minnesota, and Oregon have passed similar notice laws for large warehouse employers. If you received one of those notices, it tells you exactly how your work was measured, and your own results against it are honest resume numbers.

If no one ever gave you a number, estimate from a normal shift, round it down, and mark it as approximate. A manager would rather see "about 100 units an hour" than a precise figure you cannot explain in the interview.

## Warehouse worker resume skills that carry weight

**Scanners and systems.** Name the RF handheld scanners, voice picking headsets, pick-to-light and put-to-light systems you used, and the warehouse management system (WMS) by name if you know it, such as Manhattan, Blue Yonder, SAP, Oracle, or the company's own system. Knowing the system shortens your training, and recruiters often search for those names.

**Equipment.** List the equipment you ran, such as manual pallet jacks, electric pallet jacks, order pickers, reach trucks, sit-down forklifts, carts, and conveyors. An electric pallet jack counts as a powered industrial truck under OSHA's rule, so the training and evaluation your employer gave you are worth naming, along with the truck type.

**Receiving and inventory.** Describe receiving work such as unloading trucks, checking shipments against the paperwork, putaway, cycle counts, and inventory adjustments in the system. Cycle-count accuracy is a number worth writing.

**Packing and shipping.** Describe packing to standard, choosing box sizes, labeling, building pallets, wrapping loads, loading trailers, and working with bills of lading and carrier pickups.

**Stocking and replenishment.** Show that you kept pick locations full, rotated stock first in, first out, and handled temperature-controlled or hazardous products if your building carried them.

## Moving up from the floor

The lines that point upward matter even for an entry-level job: training new hires, covering a lead's duties, running a cycle-count team, earning a second piece of equipment, or moving from picking into receiving or inventory control. Each one shows the manager that you can be trusted with more. ## Format notes

**Length follows your history.** A warehouse worker with one or two jobs usually fits on one page, and that is fine. **Two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive job to describe,** such as several warehouses plus a military or retail history. Keep the numbers, the equipment, the systems, and the safety record; cut the duties every warehouse job shares.

Use reverse chronological order and a single column, and leave out graphics and photos. Large warehouse employers and staffing agencies run applications through hiring software, and a two-column template can scramble the read. List staffing-agency jobs under the agency's name with the warehouse you worked at, for example "[Staffing agency], assigned to [warehouse]," so the dates make sense.

Gaps get one plain dated line. Seasonal and peak-season work is normal in this industry, and listing it honestly with its dates reads well.

## Warehouse worker resume examples: a bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Picked about [N] [units/lines] an hour on an RF scanner at [N]% accuracy; met or beat the standard rate [N] weeks in a row"
- "Packed [N] orders a shift to standard; [N]% pack accuracy across [peak season/the year]"
- "Unloaded and received [N] trucks a shift; checked shipments against the paperwork and put away product in [WMS]"
- "Ran cycle counts on [N] locations a week at [N]% count accuracy"
- "Trained and evaluated on the [electric pallet jack/order picker/reach truck] by [employer], [year]"
- "Built and wrapped [N] pallets a shift and loaded [N] trailers for carrier pickup"
- "[N] months with no missed shifts; worked every peak-season overtime shift offered"
- "Trained [N] new hires on the pick line and covered the lead's duties on [N] shifts"

## What screening software looks for on a warehouse worker resume

Filters commonly read for: warehouse associate, warehouse worker, picker, packer, material handler, order picking, packing, shipping, receiving, putaway, inventory, cycle count, RF scanner, voice picking, pick-to-light, WMS, pallet jack, electric pallet jack, forklift, order picker, reach truck, loading, unloading, pallet building, FIFO, accuracy, units per hour, safety, OSHA, and peak season. [O*NET's profile for stockers and order fillers](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/53-7065.00) has the full task list, and hand material movers have [their own profile](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/53-7062.00).

The highest-risk lines are the ones a warehouse can check in a phone call: a rate your last building will not confirm, equipment you were never evaluated on, or a job title changed to match the posting. Write what you actually did and how you were measured.
