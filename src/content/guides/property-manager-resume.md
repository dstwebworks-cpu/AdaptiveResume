Property managers are hired on numbers that owners and regional managers watch every month: occupancy, collections, renewals, how fast units turn, and how close the property ran to budget. A resume that says "responsible for leasing, rent collection, and maintenance" hides every one of those numbers behind a job description.

The fix is to lead with the size of the property you ran and the results you can show, then name your license, your credentials, and your software the way a hiring manager will search for them.

## Lead with your portfolio and your numbers

**Before:** "Property Manager responsible for leasing, rent collection, and maintenance."

**After:** "Property Manager, 312-unit garden community: raised occupancy from 91% to 96% in 10 months, held delinquency under 2% of monthly rent, renewed 58% of expiring leases, and ran a $1.4M operating budget in Yardi."

The second version tells the reader the size and type of property in the first five words, then gives four numbers an owner recognizes. Every one of them comes from reports a property manager already runs.

## The numbers an owner or regional manager reads

- **Size and type.** Units or doors for residential, square feet for commercial, and the property type: garden, mid-rise, single-family portfolio, student, senior, affordable, office, retail, or industrial. If you ran more than one site, give the count.
- **Occupancy.** Where it was when you started and where it was when you left, or your average across the year.
- **Collections.** Delinquency as a share of monthly rent, or the share of rent collected on time.
- **Renewals.** The share of expiring leases that renewed. A strong renewal rate saves an owner the cost of turning units.
- **Turn time.** How many days a unit sat between residents, and how many make-readies your team finished in a month.
- **Maintenance.** Work orders completed, how quickly urgent ones were handled, and any capital projects you oversaw, with the dollar amount if you know it.
- **Budget.** The operating budget you managed and how the property finished against it. Net operating income is useful too, but write the part you drove, such as cutting a contract cost, rather than claiming a market-wide rise in rents.
- **People.** The staff you supervised, the vendors you managed, and the residents or tenants you served.

## Licenses and credentials, named exactly

**State licensing.** Whether a property manager needs a real estate license depends on the state and the work. The California Department of Real Estate has said there is no "property manager" license in California, and that leasing, renting, or collecting rent for an owner for pay generally requires a real estate broker license, or a salesperson working under a broker. Owners managing their own property do not need one. Other states set their own rules, so check with your state's real estate commission, and if you hold a license, write it with the state: "Real Estate Salesperson License, California."

**Industry credentials.** Name each one with its issuer, the way the certificate prints it:

- **CALP (Certified Apartment Leasing Professional),** from the National Apartment Association Education Institute (NAAEI). The [CALP requirements](https://naahq.org/naaei/credentials/calp) are at least six months of on-site leasing experience, seven courses, and an exam within six months of becoming a candidate.
- **CAM (Certified Apartment Manager),** also from NAAEI. It requires at least 12 months of on-site property management experience, 40 hours of coursework, and an exam passed within six months.
- **CPM (Certified Property Manager),** from the Institute of Real Estate Management (IREM). It requires 36 months of qualifying real estate management experience, eight courses, and a two-part capstone that includes the certification exam.
- **CMCA (Certified Manager of Community Associations),** from the Community Association Managers International Certification Board (CAMICB), for managers of homeowners and condominium associations.

If you are working toward one, write it as in progress with an expected date: "CAM candidate, NAAEI, expected 06/2027."

**Fair housing training.** The federal Fair Housing Act, enforced in part by the [Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/crt/fair-housing-act-1), bars housing discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, and disability, and many states and cities add protections of their own. Write the training you completed, the provider, and the year. It matters most for anyone who shows units or screens applications.

## Leasing agents and leasing consultants

Leasing is often the first job in property management, and the resume should lead with leasing numbers:

- tours given per week or month
- applications processed
- leases signed, and the share of tours that became leases
- renewals you handled
- how quickly you followed up with prospects, if your system tracked it

Name your CALP if you have it, your fair housing training, and the software you used for leads and applications. If you are moving up to assistant manager, add the work you did beyond leasing: collections calls, move-in and move-out inspections, or covering the office when the manager was out.

## Residential, commercial, and community associations

The numbers change with the property type, so match them to the jobs you want:

- **Commercial property** leans on square footage, lease administration, tenant improvement projects, and the yearly reconciliation of shared operating costs billed back to tenants.
- **Community associations** lean on board meetings, annual budgets and reserves, vendor contracts, and the rules and violation process the board sets.
- **Affordable housing** adds program compliance: income certifications, file audits, and inspections. Name the program and your role in it.

If your experience is in hotels, our [hotel manager resume guide](/guides/hotel-manager-resume/) covers that path; hotels use different numbers, such as average daily rate and revenue per available room.

## Software and skills

Name the property management software you ran daily, such as Yardi, RealPage, AppFolio, Entrata, Buildium, Rent Manager, or MRI, and the parts of it you used: leasing, accounting, work orders, or reporting. Many postings ask for a specific system, and a recruiter's search for that name finds the resumes that list it.

Skills worth naming, when they are true: budgeting and variance reports, lease enforcement, resident relations, vendor bidding and contracts, make-ready scheduling, rent collection, notices handled with the owner's attorney, inspections, and staff training.

## Format notes

- **Use the title your employer used,** and match the posting's wording in your summary. "Community Manager" and "Property Manager" are often the same job, and O*NET lists both among the common titles for the work.
- **Put the property size next to the title** so a reader sees the scale before the bullets.
- **Group several sites under one employer** with the total, then a line for each site if the numbers differ.
- **Keep the layout plain,** with standard headings and no tables, so screening software reads every line.

## Property manager resume bullets you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Managed a [number]-unit [type] community with [number] staff; raised occupancy from [X]% to [Y]% in [number] months."
- "Held delinquency under [X]% of monthly rent by [your method, such as weekly collection calls and payment plans]."
- "Renewed [X]% of expiring leases over [period]."
- "Cut average turn time from [X] to [Y] days by [your change]."
- "Ran a $[amount] operating budget and finished [X]% under budget on [expense line]."
- "Leasing: gave [number] tours a month and signed [number] leases, a [X]% closing rate."

## What screening software looks for on a property manager resume

Filters commonly read for: property manager, community manager, assistant property manager, leasing consultant, leasing agent, occupancy, leasing, renewals, retention, delinquency, collections, rent roll, budget, variance, NOI, make-ready, turns, work orders, preventive maintenance, vendor management, fair housing, resident relations, lease enforcement, inspections, CAM, CALP, CPM, CMCA, real estate license, Yardi, RealPage, AppFolio, Entrata, Buildium, and MRI. [O*NET's profile for property, real estate, and community association managers](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/11-9141.00) has the full task list.

The lines most worth checking are the ones an owner can verify from a report: an occupancy figure you never reached, a budget you did not control, or a credential you have not finished. Write what you actually did.
