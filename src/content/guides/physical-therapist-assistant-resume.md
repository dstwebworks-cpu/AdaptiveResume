Physical therapist assistant (PTA) hiring runs on one question: can the supervising physical therapist (PT) hand you their caseload and trust you to carry the plan of care, progress it within the plan, and flag the patient who is not responding? Generic language ("provided quality patient care in a fast-paced rehabilitation setting") doesn't answer that. The specifics do — your settings, the diagnoses you have treated, the caseload you carried, your license exactly as your state issues it, and the charting system you documented in.

The other thing that trips up PTA resumes is scope blur in both directions. Some pages claim evaluation and diagnosis, which belong to the PT and read as a red flag. Others shrink the job to "assisted the physical therapist" and hide real, skilled treatment work. Say what your setting was, what you treated, and what you did under the plan of care — no more, no less.

## The caseload line: your scope in one sentence

Under each role, give one line a fellow PTA or a rehab director would recognize instantly.

**Before:** "Physical therapist assistant responsible for treating patients and assisting the physical therapist with rehabilitation."

**After:** "PTA, [outpatient orthopedic] clinic; [12–14] patients per day under PT plans of care — post-surgical knee and shoulder, low back, and balance cases; manual therapy, therapeutic exercise, and modalities; [WebPT] documentation."

## Credentials, exactly as held

Every item in this block gets checked against a state board lookup, so it has to be exactly right. Our guide on [how to list certifications on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume/) covers placement and format; here is what typically belongs in a PTA block:

- **State PTA license or certification** — issued by your state's physical therapy board, with the state and license number. Every state and the District of Columbia licenses or certifies PTAs; the term and the requirements vary by state, so use your board's exact wording.
- **NPTE-PTA** — the physical therapist assistant version of the National Physical Therapy Examination, developed and administered by FSBPT (Federation of State Boards of Physical Therapy). A short line with the month and year passed is enough.
- **CAPTE-accredited program** — graduation from a PTA program accredited by CAPTE (Commission on Accreditation in Physical Therapy Education). Name the school and the degree; boards and many employers look for the accreditation.
- **BLS** — Basic Life Support, typically through the American Heart Association, with current-through dates.
- **PT Compact privilege** — the Physical Therapy Compact lets PTAs and PTs who live in and hold a license from an active member state purchase a "compact privilege" to practice in other member states. If you hold privileges, list each state — home health and travel employers filter for this.
- **APTA membership and PTA Advanced Proficiency Pathways** — the APTA (American Physical Therapy Association) runs the PTA Advanced Proficiency Pathways program for its members, an assessment-based certificate in one of eight areas: acute care, cardiovascular and pulmonary, geriatrics, neurology, oncology, orthopedics, pediatrics, and wound management. If you have completed one, name the area and the year.

List exactly what you hold, with real status. Nothing you're "about to take" unless it's labeled that way — "NPTE-PTA, scheduled [month/year]; license application pending" is honest and reads fine. A misnamed credential is the fastest way to lose ground in a profession where every license is looked up before an offer.

## The parts of the job that carry the most weight

**Treatment under the plan of care.** This is the center of the job. Say it plainly: delivered treatment under PT plans of care, progressed interventions within the plan, and reported status changes to the supervising PT. That wording shows you know the supervision line, which is the first thing a rehab director listens for.

**Settings.** Outpatient orthopedics, SNF (skilled nursing facility), acute care, inpatient rehabilitation, home health, pediatrics, and sports are different jobs with different caseloads, documentation, and pace. Name each setting you have worked and what the caseload looked like there.

**Diagnoses and populations.** Post-surgical joint replacement, rotator cuff repair, low back pain, stroke and other neurological cases, balance and fall-risk patients, pediatric developmental delay, cardiac and pulmonary patients. Write what you have actually treated, not what the clinic sees.

**Interventions and modalities.** Therapeutic exercise, gait training, transfer training, the manual therapy techniques you were trained in, and modalities (machine-based treatments) by name — electrical stimulation, ultrasound, moist heat and cold, mechanical traction. Screening software keys on the nouns.

**Outcome measures.** The tools you have administered and recorded: Timed Up and Go (TUG), Berg Balance Scale, Six-Minute Walk Test, Lower Extremity Functional Scale (LEFS), Oswestry Disability Index. Listing them shows you track progress, not just deliver sessions.

**Documentation and Medicare.** Daily notes and progress toward plan-of-care goals. Medicare requires the CQ modifier on outpatient physical therapy services furnished in whole or in part by a PTA, and since 01/01/2022 those claims are paid at 85 percent of the otherwise applicable Part B amount — so clinics care that PTAs document minutes accurately. If you handled that, say so.

**Patient and family education.** Home exercise programs, safe transfers, fall prevention, and equipment use.

**Charting.** WebPT, Net Health (which acquired Casamba), Epic, or Oracle Health (formerly Cerner) by name.

## If you're an occupational therapy assistant

The same rules apply to an occupational therapy assistant (OTA) resume, with the credentials swapped. The national credential is **COTA (Certified Occupational Therapy Assistant)** from NBCOT (National Board for Certification in Occupational Therapy), plus your state license or certification as the state issues it. Your caseload line names the setting, the populations, and the work under the OT's plan of care: activities of daily living training, upper-extremity rehabilitation, splinting, and adaptive equipment. Medicare's parallel to the CQ modifier is the CO modifier for services furnished in whole or in part by an OTA. Everything else on this page carries over as written.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** Two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive clinical role to describe; one page is right for new graduates listing clinical rotations. Order matters more than length: license and certification block near the top in its own labeled section (screening software keys on it), then experience with a caseload line atop each role. If you're trimming to fit, cut older non-clinical detail before you cut a single treatment specific.

One genuine exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change. That's a hard requirement, not a style preference.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, standard headings, real text rather than graphics or tables — hospital and clinic systems parse plain layouts most reliably. Skip the graphics.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "PTA, [setting]; [N] patients per day under PT plans of care; [diagnoses/populations treated]"
- "Progressed therapeutic exercise, gait, and transfer training within the plan of care; reported status changes to the supervising PT"
- "Delivered [modalities: electrical stimulation, ultrasound, moist heat] per plan of care in an [outpatient/SNF/acute] setting"
- "Administered and recorded [TUG, Berg Balance Scale, Six-Minute Walk Test] at [interval] to track progress toward goals"
- "Documented daily notes and progress in [system]; applied the CQ modifier and tracked treatment minutes for Medicare Part B claims"
- "Instructed patients and families in home exercise programs, safe transfers, and fall prevention"
- "Treated [N] home health visits per week across [county/area]; coordinated with the supervising PT on plan-of-care changes"
- "Mentored [N] PTA students through clinical rotations; oriented new hires to [system] documentation"

Each bullet is a frame, not a claim. If a line isn't true of your practice, it doesn't go on your page — an interviewer's first follow-up question will find it.

## What screening software looks for on a physical therapist assistant resume

Clinic and hospital filters read for the vocabulary of the profession, and a human reads for the same words minutes later. The terms that typically matter: physical therapist assistant, PTA, plan of care, therapeutic exercise, gait training, transfer training, manual therapy, modalities, electrical stimulation, ultrasound, outpatient orthopedics, skilled nursing facility, SNF, acute care, home health, pediatrics, home exercise program, CQ modifier, NPTE-PTA, CAPTE, BLS, PT Compact, WebPT, Net Health, and Epic.

The occupation has its own code: [O*NET profiles this work under Physical Therapist Assistants (31-2021.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/31-2021.00). Make sure your license line and caseload line hit that target.
