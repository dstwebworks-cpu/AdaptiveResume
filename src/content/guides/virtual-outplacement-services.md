Virtual outplacement solved one problem and created another. The problem it solved was geography: a company with people in fourteen states could not send them all to an office, so the office became a login. The problem it created is that a login is easy to ignore. A departing employee who was going to sit across a desk from a coach now gets an email with a link, and a week later the provider's report says whether they clicked it.

The result is that a virtual program lives or dies on two things: whether the first step is something the employee can finish in one sitting, and whether the resume that comes out of it is any good. Everything else in the package is furniture.

## What a virtual program should include

**A resume that is rebuilt, not templated.** In a virtual program the resume is often the only deliverable the employee holds in their hands, and it is the one they send out. It has to be built from what the person actually did, with every line confirmed by them, not their old resume dropped into a new layout. The section below says how to check.

**Coaching that is scheduled, not offered.** A remote program that says "coaching available on request" delivers very little coaching. One that books the first session in the welcome email delivers some. Ask which one the proposal is.

**A platform that does one thing well.** Job listings, a networking tool, practice interviews, and document storage are all fine. What matters is whether the employee can find the one thing they need that week without a tour. Ask to see the first screen.

**A human on the other end of the login.** Someone who answers within a business day when a remote employee has a question. Distributed teams are used to asynchronous work, but a laid-off employee waiting three days for a reply from a portal reads it as abandonment.

**Reporting that counts finished work.** Resumes completed and sessions attended, by person, not logins.

## What outplacement software does, and does not do

Outplacement software is the platform: it schedules, stores, lists jobs, tracks activity, and reports. Those are real jobs, and that is what the software is for. What it does not do is find what an employee left off their resume. The platform can hold a template and it can hold a finished document; the work between the two is done by a writer, by the employee alone, or, in the newer services, by software that drafts the resume.

That last option is where a buyer should look hardest. Software-drafted resumes are fast and consistent, and they can also be confidently wrong: a credential the employee does not hold, a scope they never had, a title inflated to match the target role. The question to ask any provider is not "do you use software" but "how does the system know a line is true before it goes on the page." If the answer is "the writer reviews it" or "the employee can edit it afterward," the program is relying on the employee to catch errors in a document they are stressed and in a hurry to send.

## How to check the resume piece in a virtual program

Ask the provider for a sample of what an employee receives, then check four things:

1. **Does the resume contain anything the old one did not?** If the sample is the old resume restyled, the program reformats. Price it as formatting.
2. **Was each line confirmed by the employee?** Ask how. A rebuilt resume should come with a record of what was proposed and what the person confirmed.
3. **How were licenses and certifications handled?** They should be asked about, never assumed from a job title.
4. **Does it read cleanly to screening software?** Single column, standard headings, no graphics or photos. A virtual program that hands back a two-column template has handed the employee a file the next employer's software may scramble.

Here is the difference on one line. This is a sample worker, not a real person.

**Before:** "Customer support team lead. Managed a team and handled escalations."

**After:** "Customer support team lead for a 14-person remote team across three time zones: ran the queue in Zendesk at 1,900 tickets a week, held first-response time under 2 hours and satisfaction at 93%, trained 6 new hires on the escalation playbook, and cut repeat contacts by 18% in one year."

In a real rebuild, the team size, the system, the volumes, and the results would all be true and all missing, and a template would have kept the first line.

## A week-one checklist for the employer

A remote program is decided in its first week, before the usage report exists. Five things an HR lead can check without waiting for the provider:

1. **The welcome message went out within one business day of the notification**, and it says what happens next in plain words. Ask to be copied on it.
2. **The first step is finishable in one sitting.** If the first thing an employee is asked to do is complete a 40-field profile, expect many of them to stop there.
3. **The resume work has started.** In a program that rebuilds resumes, employees are answering questions about their real work in week one. In a program that templates, they are uploading a file and waiting.
4. **A human answered the first question.** Send a test question through the employee-facing channel and time the reply. One business day is a fair standard to hold a provider to.
5. **Someone at your company knows the numbers the report will use.** Decide now that "resumes completed" and "sessions attended" are the two you will read, and tell the provider.

## Reporting you can hold a provider to

Ask for the report format before you sign, and ask for three columns per person: resume completed (yes or no, with the date), coaching sessions attended, and last activity. Ask that logins not be counted as activity. A provider who will not report resumes completed by person is telling you something about how much the resume piece matters to them. For the resume piece on its own, the first column is the one that applies, and it is the one we deliver.

## Format notes

Two pages is normal once a person has more than one substantive role, and a remote program has no reason to force a shorter file. Keep it reverse chronological, single column, with no photos or graphics. Anyone in the group applying to federal jobs through USAJOBS is capped at two pages under the OPM change effective 09/27/2025.
