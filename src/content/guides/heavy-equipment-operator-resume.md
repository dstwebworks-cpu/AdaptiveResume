Heavy equipment hiring is done by people who have run the machines themselves. The superintendent reading your resume wants to know one thing: can they put you in a [50]-ton excavator on a live utility trench tomorrow morning and trust the grade, the swing, and the judgment? Generic language ("operated heavy equipment on construction sites in a safe and efficient manner") doesn't answer that. The specifics do — which machines, what size, what grade-control system, what kind of work, and the safety training you hold.

The other thing that costs operators is scope vagueness. Site work, road building, utility trenching, demolition, and aggregate loading are related but different jobs, and a resume that just says "equipment operator" competes badly against one that names the machines and the work.

## The machine line: your scope in one sentence

Under each role, give one line a fellow operator would recognize instantly.

**Before:** "Operated heavy equipment on construction sites and followed all safety procedures."

**After:** "Excavator and dozer operator on [civil site-work] crews; ran [Caterpillar 336]-class excavators and [D6]-class dozers with [Trimble Earthworks] grade control; [N] cubic yards moved and [N] linear feet of storm pipe set per [month]; CDL Class A; incident-free since [year]."

## Credentials and training, exactly as held

Every item in this block gets checked at onboarding. Our guide on [how to list certifications on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume/) covers placement and format; here is what typically belongs in an operator's block:

- **CDL Class A** — the commercial driver's license class for combination vehicles: a gross combination weight rating of 26,001 pounds or more with a towed unit rated over 10,000 pounds, under FMCSA (Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration) rules. This is what lets you haul your own machine on a lowboy, and many contractors filter for it.
- **OSHA 10-hour or 30-hour Construction** — cards from the OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) Outreach Training Program. OSHA itself says the card is training, not a certification or license, so write it as "OSHA 30-Hour Construction card, [year]" and nothing grander.
- **MSHA Part 46 or Part 48 training** — required by the Mine Safety and Health Administration before you work at a mine. Part 46 covers sand, gravel, surface stone, and similar surface operations; Part 48 covers coal, underground mines, and surface metal mines. Name the part you actually trained under and the year of your latest annual refresher.
- **NCCCO crane operator certification** — only if you run cranes. On construction sites, OSHA's 29 CFR 1926.1427 requires crane operators to be certified by an accredited testing organization, by crane type (or type and capacity), and the certification runs five years. NCCCO (National Commission for the Certification of Crane Operators) is the best-known of the accredited bodies; NCCER and the Operating Engineers Certification Program also certify. If you don't run cranes, leave this off.
- **NCCER Heavy Equipment Operations** — the National Center for Construction Education and Research offers a three-level curriculum and credentials through its accredited training programs. List the level completed.
- **IUOE apprenticeship** — the International Union of Operating Engineers runs registered apprenticeship programs through its local training centers, leading to journey-level operating engineer. Name the local and the completion year.
- **Manufacturer or dealer training** — Caterpillar, Komatsu, John Deere, and Volvo courses, and dealer-run grade-control training. Name the course and machine family.
- **Competent person training for excavations** — under 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, a competent person classifies the soil, inspects the trench daily, and has the authority to stop work. If you have been designated, say so with the year.

List exactly what you hold, with real status. Nothing you're "about to take" unless it's labeled that way — "NCCCO mobile crane, exam scheduled [month/year]" is honest and reads fine. A misnamed credential is the fastest way to lose ground in a trade where the safety manager checks every card before you're allowed on site.

## The parts of the job that carry the most weight

**Machines and sizes.** This is the center of the resume. Name each machine type and its size class: excavators by ton class, dozers by model class, wheel loaders by bucket size, plus graders, haul trucks, skid steers, backhoes, and compactors. Years on each matters more than a long list; a [30]-ton excavator and a mini excavator are different jobs.

**Grade control and GPS.** Machine-control systems are a hard filter on many crews now. Name the system by its real name — Trimble Earthworks, Topcon MC-X, or whatever your machine carried — and say whether you ran 2D or 3D, and whether you loaded models or just worked from them. Grade checking with a rover or laser is a separate skill worth its own line.

**Trench safety and utility locates.** Utility work is where operators get hurt. Say what you have actually done: worked inside protective systems (trench boxes, sloping, benching), potholed to expose marked lines, and worked under 811 locate tickets — 811 is the national call-before-you-dig number.

**Production.** Say what came out of the machine: cubic yards moved, linear feet of pipe set, tons loaded per shift, acres cleared, lifts placed and compacted. Write what you measured, the way the job measured it. If your crew didn't track a number, don't invent one.

**Pre-shift inspections and maintenance.** Daily walk-arounds, fluid checks, grease points, undercarriage checks, and the write-ups you turned in. Operators who keep machines running are worth more than operators who only run them.

**Safety record.** Incident-free hours or years, if your employer tracked them, and any toolbox talks you led. State it as your record, not as a promise.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** Two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive operating role to describe; one page is right for apprentices or people moving up from laborer. Order matters more than length: credential and training block near the top in its own labeled section (screening software keys on it), then experience with a machine line atop each role. If you're trimming to fit, cut older non-operating detail before you cut a single machine or system.

One genuine exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change. That's a hard requirement, not a style preference.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, standard headings, real text rather than graphics or tables — contractor and staffing systems parse plain layouts most reliably. Skip the graphics.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Excavator operator, [N]-ton class ([Caterpillar/Komatsu]); [N] years on [utility trenching/mass excavation/demolition]"
- "Ran [Trimble Earthworks/Topcon MC-X] 3D grade control on [dozer/excavator]; finished subgrade to [tolerance] on [N] projects"
- "Moved [N] cubic yards of [cut/fill] on a [N]-acre site; set [N] linear feet of [storm/sanitary/water] pipe"
- "Worked inside trench boxes and sloped excavations under 811 locate tickets; designated competent person, [year]"
- "Loaded [N] haul trucks per hour with a [N]-yard wheel loader; MSHA Part 46 trained, refresher [year]"
- "Hauled own equipment on a lowboy between sites; CDL Class A, [state]"
- "Completed daily pre-shift inspections and reported [N] defects for repair"
- "Trained [N] new operators on [machine]; led weekly toolbox talks for a [N]-person crew"

Each bullet is a frame, not a claim. If a line isn't true of your work, it doesn't go on your page — a superintendent's first follow-up question will find it.

## What screening software looks for on a heavy equipment operator resume

Contractor filters read for the vocabulary of the trade, and a human reads for the same words minutes later. The terms that typically matter: heavy equipment operator, excavator, dozer, bulldozer, wheel loader, motor grader, backhoe, skid steer, haul truck, grade control, machine control, GPS, Trimble, Topcon, grade checking, trenching, trench box, competent person, utility locate, 811, cubic yards, site work, civil, demolition, aggregate, CDL Class A, OSHA 10, OSHA 30, MSHA Part 46, NCCCO, NCCER, and IUOE.

[O*NET profiles this work under Operating Engineers and Other Construction Equipment Operators (47-2073.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/47-2073.00). One honesty note: crane operators are coded separately, so if you split time between earthmoving and cranes, name both plainly and let the credential block carry the crane certification. The highest-risk lines are the ones that overstate scope — a machine you ran once written as one you run daily, an OSHA card written as a certification, a crane you helped rig written as a crane you operated. Construction is a small world, and employers check.
