Start at the top with your unemployment claim, then work down. If you were given a severance agreement, read that section early, because its deadline can come before your 60 days for health coverage run out.

This guide is general information, not legal or financial advice. Rules change and states differ, so for your own situation, ask the agency named in each section or an employment attorney.

## Days 1 to 7: file for unemployment

Unemployment insurance is a joint state and federal program, and each state runs its own. According to the [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/unemployment-insurance), you file with the program in the state where you worked, and you should contact it as soon as possible after becoming unemployed. The Department says the first payment generally arrives two to three weeks after you file, so a claim filed early means an earlier first payment.

Your state's site lists what it asks for and how to apply. Our [state-by-state list of official unemployment sites](/guides/how-to-file-for-unemployment-by-state/) links each one directly.

Here are two things to know once you file:

- **Benefits are taxable.** The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc418) says you generally must include unemployment compensation in your income. You can choose to have federal income tax withheld from your benefits by filing Form W-4V.
- **Your state may ask about your job search.** Your state will tell you whether you must record your job-search contacts and how to report them. Our free [Excel job search tracker](/guides/job-search-tracker-and-work-search-log/) keeps those contacts, dates and follow-ups in one place.

## Days 1 to 60: choose your health coverage

This is the decision with the hardest deadlines, and two programs are involved.

**COBRA** lets you keep your former employer's group health plan for a while. The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/laws-and-regulations/laws/cobra) says it usually lasts up to 18 months and can cover the family members who were on your plan. It applies to [group health plans sponsored by employers with 20 or more employees](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/health-plans/cobra), and you may have to pay the whole premium plus up to 2% more. The Department's guide notes that this is often more than you're used to paying, because employers usually pay part of the cost for active employees. [Some states have similar laws](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/publications/an-employees-guide-health-benefits-under-cobra-2022.pdf) for employers with fewer than 20 employees; your state insurance commissioner's office can tell you whether one covers you.

**If you are 65 or older, or will be soon,** ask Medicare about enrolling before you choose COBRA. The Department's guide warns that choosing COBRA instead of Medicare can mean a late enrollment penalty and a gap in coverage if you later want Part B. It also says that if you lose your job after your Medicare initial enrollment period and did not enroll in Part A or B, you generally have eight months to sign up. That window starts the month after your job ends or the month after your group health coverage ends, whichever is earlier.

The Department's [Employee's Guide to Health Benefits Under COBRA](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/publications/an-employees-guide-health-benefits-under-cobra-2022.pdf) sets out this timeline:

- Your employer has 30 days after your job ends to notify the plan.
- The plan then has 14 days to send you an election notice.
- You have 60 days to elect COBRA, counted from when your coverage ends or when you receive the notice, whichever is later.
- After you elect, you have at least 45 days to make the first premium payment.

**A Marketplace plan** is the other path. The Department of Labor's guide says you can choose a plan from 60 days before to 60 days after you lose job-based coverage, and [HealthCare.gov](https://www.healthcare.gov/unemployed/marketplace-options/) explains your options. Being offered COBRA does not make you ineligible for a Marketplace plan or for the tax credit that lowers its premiums. You can look at Marketplace plans at HealthCare.gov or by calling 1-800-318-2596, and apply the same way. Compare them with what COBRA would cost you before you choose.

**Watch for one mistake.** If you take COBRA and then drop it on your own, you generally can't switch to a Marketplace plan until open enrollment, according to [HealthCare.gov](https://www.healthcare.gov/coverage-outside-open-enrollment/special-enrollment-period/). When COBRA runs out on its own, you get 60 days to enroll. The Department of Labor's guide also describes one way some people bridge the gap: electing COBRA to cover medical care until a Marketplace plan begins.

## Your final paycheck and unused time off

The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/wages/lastpaycheck) says federal law does not require an employer to hand over the final paycheck immediately, but some states do. If your regular payday passes and you have not been paid, contact the Department's Wage and Hour Division or your state labor department.

Unused vacation is a separate question. Federal law does [not require payment for time not worked](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/workhours/vacation_leave), such as vacation, and the Department of Labor says vacation benefits are a matter of agreement between you and your employer. Check the policy or agreement you were given, and ask your state labor department whether your state has its own rules on paying out unused vacation.

## Before you sign a severance agreement

A severance agreement usually pays you in exchange for releasing legal claims against the employer. If you are 40 or older and it asks you to give up age discrimination claims, federal law ([29 U.S.C. 626(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/626)) sets minimum terms:

- The agreement must advise you in writing to consult an attorney before you sign.
- You get at least 21 days to consider it, or at least 45 days if the layoff is part of a program offered to a group of employees.
- After you sign, you have at least 7 days to revoke, and the agreement does not take effect until those 7 days pass.
- In a group program, the employer must also tell you in writing which employees the program covers, what makes someone eligible, and any time limits. It must list the job titles and ages of everyone eligible or selected, and the ages of those in the same job classification or unit who were not.

The [EEOC](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/qa-understanding-waivers-discrimination-claims-employee-severance-agreements) explains these rules in plain language. Whatever your age, read the agreement's terms on returning company property and keeping company information confidential. Return what belongs to the employer, keep nothing from its systems, and ask an employment attorney about any term you are unsure of before you sign.

**Was there advance notice?** Under the federal WARN Act ([29 U.S.C. 2102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/2102)), an employer with 100 or more employees generally must give 60 days' written notice before a plant closing or mass layoff, with exceptions such as business circumstances that could not reasonably have been foreseen. Whether it applies to your layoff depends on the numbers involved, so if you think notice was owed and not given, ask your state labor department or an attorney.

## Before you cash out your 401(k)

When you leave a job, your former employer's retirement plan may offer to pay out your balance. Before you take it as cash, know the cost:

- **Taxes, plus an extra 10%.** The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc558) charges an extra 10% tax on the taxable part of an early distribution, taken before age 59½. One exception covers money paid from that employer's plan after you leave the employer at age 55 or older. The IRS says this exception applies to plans other than an IRA, so it no longer applies once you move the money into an IRA.
- **20% held back.** A plan distribution paid to you is [subject to mandatory withholding of 20%](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/rollovers-of-retirement-plan-and-ira-distributions), even if you intend to roll it over later.
- **A 60-day window.** You have 60 days from the date you receive a distribution to roll it into another plan or an IRA. To roll over the full amount, you must use other money to make up the 20% that was withheld. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/rollovers-of-retirement-plan-and-ira-distributions) says that if you ask the plan to pay another plan or an IRA directly, no taxes are withheld from the transfer.

Ask the plan how each choice would be paid before you request anything, and talk with a tax professional if the amount matters to your budget this year.

## Public help near you

There are about 2,300 [American Job Centers](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/american-job-centers) across the country. They offer job search help, training referrals, career counseling, and re-employment services for people on unemployment. Services vary by state and some have eligibility rules, so call or visit your local center to see what it offers. To find one, use the [American Job Center Finder](https://www.careeronestop.org/LocalHelp/AmericanJobCenters/find-american-job-centers.aspx) or call 1-877-US-2JOBS. If retraining is on your mind, ask about the Dislocated Worker program under the federal Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA). The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/workforce-investment/adult) says it is designed to help workers get back to work as quickly as possible, and its services are offered through American Job Centers. Our guide for [people out of work six months or more](/guides/long-term-unemployed-restarting-your-job-search/) explains who qualifies and how training is paid for.
