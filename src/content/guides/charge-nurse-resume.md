Charge is the job interviews ask about and resumes mysteriously hide. If you've run a shift — made the assignments, absorbed the call-offs, kept beds moving, taken the escalations — you've been doing a leadership job, whether or not your title ever changed. A charge nurse resume has one purpose: make that scope visible instead of leaving it buried inside "staff RN."

## What hiring managers and screening software look for on a charge nurse resume

Managers hiring for charge, house supervisor, or unit leadership read for scope words, and screening software searches for the same ones:

- **Assignment-making:** matching nurses to patients by acuity and skill mix, adjusting mid-shift as the unit changes.
- **Staffing:** working the schedule in real time — call-offs, floats, sitters — and coordinating with the house supervisor or staffing office.
- **Bed flow:** admissions, discharges, and transfers; the throughput work that keeps a unit from silting up.
- **Escalation:** being the shift's first call — rapid responses, family concerns, conflicts, equipment failures — and knowing what to resolve and what to move up the chain.
- **The steady layer:** huddles, incident reporting, precepting and mentoring, and your clinical base (unit type, certifications as held, Epic or Cerner).

For the underlying occupation vocabulary, [O*NET profiles this work under Registered Nurses (29-1141.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/29-1141.00) — charge is a leadership role within the RN occupation, not a separately coded job. That's exactly why your resume has to state the scope explicitly: no job code is going to say it for you.

## Name the scope: beds, staff, and shift

The strongest single line on a charge nurse resume is the one that sizes the job:

**Before:** "Served as charge nurse and handled various leadership duties on the unit."
**After:** "Charge nurse, [N]-bed [unit], [nights]: acuity-based assignments for [N] RNs and [N] techs; bed flow with the house supervisor; first escalation point for the shift."

Nothing was invented in the rewrite. The vague version had all the same facts and surrendered them; the rewrite states them at their real size.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Charge nurse ([permanent / rotating, frequency]), [N]-bed [unit], [shift]"
- "Made acuity-based assignments for [N] RNs and [N] support staff each shift"
- "Adjusted staffing through call-offs and census swings; coordinated floats with the staffing office"
- "Managed bed flow: admissions, discharges, and transfers in coordination with the house supervisor"
- "First escalation point for clinical concerns, family issues, and staff conflicts; escalated per chain of command when warranted"
- "Led shift huddles; communicated safety and practice updates to the oncoming shift"
- "Responded to rapid responses and codes on the unit as charge"
- "Precepted [N] new nurses; mentored [N] into their own charge rotation"
- "Completed incident reports and followed up with unit leadership on recurring issues"
- "Kept a full or modified patient assignment while in charge [if true — say which]"

That last bracket matters. Charge-with-assignment and charge-out-of-count are different workloads, and hiring managers know it. State yours as it was.

## Does a charge nurse resume need a separate leadership section?

Usually not. The cleaner pattern is to keep one experience section and put a scope line plus charge bullets at the top of each role where you held or rotated charge, so the leadership sits in context next to the clinical work it grew out of. Add a separate "Leadership" heading only if you're aiming at supervisor or manager postings and have enough material — committee work, project leads, precepting programs — to fill it honestly. Everything else follows standard nursing format: certifications in their own labeled block near the top, single column, plain headings, one to two pages.

## Aiming at supervisor or manager? Shift the emphasis, not the facts

The same record supports the next step up; what changes is which lines lead. For house supervisor and assistant manager postings, move the operational decisions forward: staffing adjustments you made and why, escalations you resolved versus escalated, throughput work with the house supervisor, and any committee or project involvement — practice councils, rollout support for a charting change, scheduling committees. Keep the clinical base visible underneath; nursing leadership hires people who can still read the unit. And resist the temptation to retitle yourself: "charge nurse" with real scope lines outranks an inflated title every time, because the person interviewing you will ask exactly what you ran.
