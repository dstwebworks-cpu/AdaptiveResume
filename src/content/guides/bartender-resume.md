Your bartender resume should answer three questions early on. What kind of bar did you work in? How much volume did you handle? And is your alcohol certification current for your state? A resume that says "made drinks and provided excellent customer service" answers none of those questions.

A craft cocktail bar, a nightclub, a hotel lobby, and a stadium need very different bartenders. The work that fits one can be a poor match for another, so say which kind of shift you have really worked. The sections below put your shifts on the page, in words a hiring manager and screening software can both find.

## The bar line: your shift in one sentence

Put one line under each job that tells a bar manager how busy your bar was.

**Before:** "Bartender responsible for making drinks and providing excellent customer service to guests."

**After:** "Bartender, [120]-seat [full-service restaurant] with a [3]-well bar; [$2,800] average sales on [Friday] shifts; [craft cocktails, draft, and wine]; opened and closed on [Toast]; [state] alcohol server certification current through [month year]."

Write your numbers from memory and from records you own, such as your own shift notes. Do not go back into a former employer's register system to collect them. If you signed a severance or confidentiality agreement, read it before you write any number, because it is a contract and breaking it can put your severance at risk. The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission [explains what it means to give up legal claims in a severance agreement](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/qa-understanding-waivers-discrimination-claims-employee-severance-agreements), and an employment attorney can tell you what yours allows.

## Alcohol certifications: know what your state requires

The rules change from state to state, so start with your own.

**Some states require their own training.** California requires on-premises servers and their managers to pass its Responsible Beverage Service certification through the state's ABC (Alcoholic Beverage Control) department. A certificate from a training company alone does not meet that requirement. Illinois requires its BASSET (Beverage Alcohol Sellers and Servers Education and Training) course for on-premises servers and anyone whose job includes checking IDs.

**Some states leave it to the employer.** Texas does not require TABC (Texas Alcoholic Beverage Commission) seller training under state law, but TABC says many employers require it. Texas does require a free opioid overdose course each year for staff at some businesses that sell alcohol, and [TABC lists who must take it](https://www.tabc.texas.gov/texas-alcohol-laws-regulations/tabc-education/). Local rules can add to any of this, so check your state's alcohol agency and your city.

**National programs are widely known.** TIPS (Training for Intervention ProcedureS), now offered by 360training, and ServSafe Alcohol, from the National Restaurant Association, are two common courses. Each state decides whether a national course counts toward its own rule, so do not assume one certificate carries across state lines.

Write each one with its end date: "[State or program] alcohol server certification, current through [month year]." If it has expired, renew it, leave it off, or write it as expired. A manager in a state with a required program needs to know you are covered.

## Drink knowledge and other credentials

These are optional, so list only what you have earned.

- **WSET.** The Wine & Spirit Education Trust offers awards by level in wine, spirits, and sake. Write the subject and the level.
- **Cicerone.** The Cicerone Certification Program begins with Certified Beer Server. It suits breweries and beer bars.
- **Sommelier training.** The Court of Master Sommeliers has its own levels. Write the exact level you passed.
- **Food handler card.** Some states and counties require one. If yours does, write it with its end date.

If you trained in-house or at a bartending school, write that plainly as training.

## Volume, drinks, and money

**Volume.** Say how many guests you served on a busy night, how many drinks per hour you managed if you know that number, and your average shift sales. Say whether you worked alone or with others, and how many wells the bar had. At a high-volume bar, start with guests served and sales per shift. At a cocktail bar, start with the drinks you built and the menu you worked on. At a restaurant or hotel bar, start with your check average and the tables you handled along with the bar.

**Your drinks.** Name what you actually made. Classic cocktails, batched programs, draft systems, wine by the glass, and non-alcoholic menus are all different skills. If you built a menu, say how many drinks were on it and who approved it.

**Responsible service.** If you checked IDs, followed your state's alcohol service laws, cut off service when needed, or followed incident procedures, say so, because those are real skills.

**Money.** Include cash handling, your drawer balance, tip-out calculations, and voids. If your drawer was consistently accurate, say so in a way you can stand behind.

**Inventory and cost.** Say whether you took inventory, placed orders, or tracked pour cost or liquor cost. If you helped lower a cost, give the before and after numbers you actually know. Tools include BevSpot and Backbar.

**Before:** "Helped with inventory and ordering."

**After:** "Took weekly inventory of [220] bottles in [BevSpot]; helped bring liquor cost from [24]% to [21]% over [6] months by [tightening pours and tracking waste]."

**Opening, closing, and training.** Say if you opened and closed the bar on your own, trained new bartenders, or supervised barbacks.

**Register systems.** Name Toast, Square, Aloha, Micros, or Lightspeed. Use the exact product name, so a hiring manager and screening software can both find it.

## Starting from barback or server

Many bartenders start as barbacks or servers, and that history is worth showing. Barbacks can list wells stocked, kegs changed, and bartenders supported during peak hours. Servers can list table counts, check averages, wine sales, and any shifts covered behind the bar.

Do not call yourself a bartender for work you did not do. If you have just finished a bartending course and have not worked a shift, say that honestly and put your service experience first.

## Format notes

Put your certifications near the top in their own labeled section, and start each job with your bar line.

Put your latest bar first, keep to one column with standard headings and real text, and skip graphics and tables so screening software can find your certification and your volume.

## Bartender bullets you can adapt, keeping only what's true

- "Bartender at a [N]-seat [bar type]; [N]-well bar; [$N] average sales on [day] shifts"
- "Served up to [N] guests per shift across [craft cocktails / draft / wine] during peak hours"
- "Built a [N]-drink seasonal menu approved by [bar manager / owner]; [N] drinks became regular menu items"
- "Checked IDs and applied [state] alcohol service rules; certified through [program], current through [month year]"
- "Took [weekly] inventory of [N] items in [system] and placed orders with [N] distributors"
- "Balanced cash drawers and calculated tip-outs for [N] staff on [Toast / Square / Aloha]"
- "Trained [N] new bartenders and supervised [N] barbacks on [opening / closing] procedures"
- "Supported [N] bartenders as a barback on [day] nights; stocked [N] wells and changed kegs during service"

Keep a bullet only if you could show a bar manager the work behind it.

## What screening software looks for on a bartender resume

Terms that often appear in bartender postings include bartender, mixology, craft cocktails, draft beer, wine service, responsible alcohol service, ID checking, TIPS, ServSafe Alcohol, cash handling, POS (point of sale), inventory, pour cost, high volume, customer service, and your systems by name. Use a term only if you have done the work behind it.

[The U.S. Department of Labor's Occupational Information Network, or O*NET, lists this work under Bartenders (35-3011.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/35-3011.00), and it's worth reading the listed tasks to catch any you did and left off.

The riskiest lines are an expired certification written as current and a title you did not hold. A manager can check both quickly by asking to see your card and calling your last bar.
