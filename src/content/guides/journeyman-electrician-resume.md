There is no such thing as a generic journeyman electrician license, and that fact should shape the whole resume. Licensing is done by states — and in some places by cities or counties — so "licensed journeyman electrician" with no issuing authority tells a contractor almost nothing. The single most useful thing you can do is state the license exactly as issued: the classification, the authority that issued it, and its status.

The second most useful thing is scope. Electrician resumes lean hard on duty lines — "installed and maintained electrical systems" — that could describe an apprentice's first month or a foreman's last decade. What an estimator or general foreman is actually trying to read is the size of work you've run: what conduit, what panels, what services, what settings, and whether anyone worked under you.

## Lead with license, scope, and setting

Under each employer, give the picture in one line before the bullets — the setting, the systems, and the scale.

**Before:** "Journeyman electrician responsible for installing and maintaining electrical systems on commercial job sites."

**After:** "Journeyman electrician ([state] license); commercial build-outs and service upgrades; bent and ran EMT and rigid conduit, installed [N] panels and [N]-amp services, led [N] apprentices; work to the NEC edition adopted in [jurisdiction]."

The second version reads in a few seconds and answers what the first leaves a hiring contractor guessing at. It names the license, the setting, the raceway types, the service size, and the crew scope — the exact language contractors write into their own postings.

## Your license, exactly as issued

Write the license the way the issuing authority writes it: the classification (journeyman, journey-level, or your jurisdiction's wording), the state or local board that issued it, and its current status. If you hold licenses in more than one state, list each one — reciprocity between states is uneven, so a contractor working across state lines reads multiple licenses as a direct advantage.

Two honest details strengthen this block:

- **The path that got you there.** A registered apprenticeship is documented, supervised experience — commonly around 8,000 on-the-job hours over four to five years, plus classroom instruction. If you came up through an IBEW/NECA program — the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers and the National Electrical Contractors Association jointly run apprenticeships through local Joint Apprenticeship and Training Committees (JATCs), under the electrical training ALLIANCE — name the local and the program (Inside Wireman is the common classification). A state-registered or employer apprenticeship is worth naming the same way.
- **What the license does not cover.** In many states, low-voltage, fire-alarm, and alarm-system work is licensed separately from the journeyman electrical license. If you hold one of those endorsements or separate licenses, list it as its own line; if you don't, don't imply it.

## The work that carries the weight

**Conduit** is the first thing a field resume gets read for. Name the raceway types you've bent and run — EMT (electrical metallic tubing), rigid, IMC (intermediate metal conduit), and PVC conduit — and the bending you actually do: hand bending, mechanical benders, hydraulic or electric benders on larger sizes. "Bent and ran EMT through [size]" is a real line; "ran conduit" is not much of one.

**Panel and service work** deserves its own lines: panel installs and changeouts, service upgrades with the amperage, distribution and subpanels, breaker and load work, terminations, and grounding and bonding. Amperage is the number that carries the information — a [N]-amp residential service and an [N]-amp commercial service are different jobs, and the reader knows it.

**Troubleshooting** is where experience shows. Say what you diagnose and with what: meters, megohmmeter testing if you do it, tracing faults in live systems under lockout/tagout procedures.

**Prints** are assumed but still worth stating plainly: blueprint reading, electrical drawings, and — for commercial and industrial work — one-line (single-line) diagrams. A journeyman who reads a one-line comfortably reads as someone who understands the system, not just the circuit in front of them.

## Residential, commercial, industrial — say which world

The three settings hire for different evidence, so name yours and write to it.

**Residential:** services and panel changes, remodels and additions, new construction rough-in and trim, generator and EV-charger installs if you do them. Volume is honest proof here — homes wired, services upgraded.

**Commercial:** tenant build-outs, three-phase distribution, lighting and lighting controls, EMT everywhere, working from one-lines and panel schedules, coordination with other trades on schedule.

**Industrial:** this is where controls knowledge separates candidates. Motor controls — starters, contactors, overloads, control circuits — plus variable frequency drives (VFDs): installing, wiring, and basic parameter setup if you do it. If you troubleshoot around programmable logic controllers (PLCs), state your real depth: reading I/O, following a ladder-logic print to find a failed input, working alongside a controls tech. "PLC troubleshooting basics — traced faults to failed I/O and field devices" is a strong, true line for a journeyman; "PLC programmer" is a different job, so don't write it unless it's yours.

## Code and safety — the block contractors check first

**The NEC.** The National Electrical Code — NFPA 70, published by the National Fire Protection Association — is revised every three years; the 2026 edition is the newest as of this writing, but states and local jurisdictions adopt editions on their own schedules, so the code enforced on your jobs may be an earlier one. The honest, useful line is "work to the NEC as adopted in [jurisdiction]," adding a specific edition year only if you've genuinely worked or trained to it — say, a code-update class for an exam.

**NFPA 70E** — the Standard for Electrical Safety in the Workplace — is the arc-flash and electrical-safety standard. If you've had 70E training, list it with the year: arc-flash boundaries, PPE categories, and energized-work permits are exactly what industrial and larger commercial employers screen for.

**OSHA cards.** OSHA 10 and OSHA 30 are completion cards from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration's Outreach Training Program — 10-hour and 30-hour construction safety courses. List the one you hold with the year. Add lockout/tagout, aerial lift or scissor-lift training, confined space, and first aid/CPR as plain lines if you have them.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** One strong page is genuinely fine for a journeyman with one or two employers — the license block and the scope detail are what earn the space, so keep those even when the page is tight. If you've run work across residential, commercial, and industrial settings, held a lead role, or carried licenses in more than one state, two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive role to describe. Cut the duty lines every electrician shares ("followed safety procedures") before you cut an amperage, a raceway type, or a license.

One exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change. That's a hard requirement, not a style preference.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, a clearly labeled license-and-certifications block near the top, and skip the graphics. A contractor wants to find your license and your scope fast, not admire a layout.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Journeyman electrician license — [issuing state/board], [status/year]"
- "Bent and ran [EMT/rigid/IMC/PVC] conduit through [size] on [commercial/industrial] jobs"
- "Installed [N] panels and completed [N] service upgrades to [N] amps"
- "Wired [single-phase/three-phase] distribution; terminated and labeled [N]-circuit panels"
- "Installed and wired motor controls and VFDs on [equipment]; traced control-circuit faults"
- "Troubleshot faults under lockout/tagout; tested with [meter/megohmmeter]"
- "Read blueprints, panel schedules, and one-line diagrams on [project type]"
- "Led [N] apprentices; laid out work from prints and coordinated with [trades/GC]"

## What screening software looks for on an electrician resume

This part applies to every electrician resume, journeyman or not. Filters commonly read for: journeyman electrician, licensed electrician, NEC, National Electrical Code, conduit, EMT, rigid conduit, conduit bending, panel installation, service upgrade, three-phase, single-phase, transformers, motor controls, VFD, PLC, troubleshooting, lockout/tagout, NFPA 70E, arc flash, OSHA 10, OSHA 30, blueprint reading, one-line diagrams, grounding and bonding, low voltage, fire alarm, rough-in, and trim. [O*NET's profile for electricians](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/47-2111.00) has the full task and tool list.

The highest-risk lines are the ones that overstate what you're licensed or qualified to do — an endorsement you don't hold, energized work you weren't qualified for, or "industrial controls" depth that's really a few service calls. License claims are checkable against a state board in minutes, and the trade is small enough that scope claims get checked informally too. Accurate detail is stronger than inflation, because it holds up.
