A line cook is hired on a handful of things a chef can picture: which stations you can run, how many covers you can handle on a busy night, how fast your tickets go out, whether you keep a clean and safe station, and whether you show up. A resume that says "cooked food and kept station clean" leaves every one of those questions open.

The fix is to name your stations, put the kitchen's own numbers on the page, and list your food safety training the way your card prints it.

## Lead with your stations and your volume

**Before:** "Line cook. Cooked food and kept station clean."

**After:** "Grill and Saute Cook, 180-seat steakhouse: ran the grill on 300-cover Friday and Saturday nights, held ticket times under 12 minutes, built the daily prep list for 2 stations, and trained 3 new cooks."

## Name your stations

Kitchens hire for stations, so list the ones you can run on a busy night without help:

- **Grill or broiler,** including the temperatures and cuts you cook to order.
- **Saute,** with the sauces and dishes you fire.
- **Fry,** including the oil changes and filtering you handle.
- **Flat-top,** for breakfast, burgers, and griddle work.
- **Pantry or garde manger,** for salads, cold apps, and desserts.
- **Prep,** with the volume you prep for a shift.
- **Expo,** if you call tickets and check plates before they leave the window.

If you have covered several stations, say so plainly: "Cross-trained on grill, saute, fry, and pantry; covered any station on the line." That one sentence tells a chef you can fill a gap on a short-staffed night.

## The numbers a chef reads

- **Seats and covers.** The size of the dining room and the covers on your busiest nights.
- **Ticket times.** If the kitchen display system tracked them, your average or your target on a rush.
- **Tickets per hour** on your station during peak service.
- **Prep volume.** How much you prepped for a shift, such as cases of produce or pounds of protein.
- **Food cost and waste,** only if the kitchen measured them and you had a part in the result.
- **Health inspections.** The kitchen's inspection results, if you worked the line through them, written as the kitchen's result, such as "Worked the line through 3 health inspections with no critical violations."
- **Attendance.** Shifts covered, doubles worked, or a record with no missed shifts, if it is true.

Write the numbers you know. A chef will believe "about 250 covers on Saturdays" from a cook who worked those Saturdays.

## Food safety, named exactly

**Food handler training.** Many states and counties require food workers to complete food handler training. The Texas Department of State Health Services, for example, says [Texas requires many food service employees](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/retail-food-establishments/licensing-food-handler-training-programs) to complete an accredited food handler course within 30 days of getting a job. Write your card the way it prints: the course name, the issuer, and the expiration date.

**Manager-level certification.** If you hold a food protection manager certificate, such as the ServSafe Food Protection Manager Certification, list it too. It can matter more as you move toward lead cook or sous chef, and our [restaurant general manager resume guide](/guides/restaurant-general-manager-resume/) covers how that certificate is written.

**Allergen and other training.** Some places require allergen awareness training (the restaurant general manager guide names two states that do), and many kitchens train on it anyway. List any training you completed with the provider and the year.

**Daily safety work.** Temperature logs, labeling and dating, first in, first out stock rotation, and cleaning and sanitizing schedules are part of the job, and naming them shows a chef you already work that way.

## Equipment and skills

Name the equipment you run, especially anything a posting asks for: flat-top, char-broiler, salamander, fryers, convection and combi ovens, steam kettles, slicers, and the kitchen display system. Add skills that set you apart, such as butchery, fish breakdown, sauces, baking, or plating to the chef's spec.

Knife skills belong on the page as specifics, such as "breaks down 20 pounds of onions and peppers for service in under an hour," rather than "excellent knife skills."

## The kitchen you worked in

Write the format and the size next to each restaurant name, because kitchens run very differently:

- fine dining, with a small menu and exact plating
- high-volume casual or diner, with big covers and fast tickets
- quick-service, with speed and consistency
- banquet and catering, with hundreds of plates at once
- hotel kitchens, often with room service and banquets
- hospital, school, and military kitchens, with set menus, diets, and large batches

If you are moving between formats, your summary can name the format you want and the skills that carry over.

## Moving up

The usual path is prep cook, line cook, lead line cook, then sous chef. To show you are ready for the next step, list the work you already do beyond your station: running the line on the sous chef's day off, writing prep lists, placing orders, counting inventory, training new cooks, and closing the kitchen.

## Format notes

- **Use the title your kitchen used,** such as Line Cook, Grill Cook, or Lead Line Cook, and name the stations right after it.
- **Name each restaurant with its city and its format.**
- **Keep short jobs honest.** Restaurant jobs often run a few months; list them with real dates, and group seasonal or event work under one heading if there are many.
- **Say when you can work** in your summary or header if it helps, such as "Available nights, weekends, and holidays." Kitchens schedule around the busiest shifts, and a chef filling a Saturday line looks for it.
- **Keep the layout plain,** with standard headings, so screening software reads every line.

## Line cook resume bullets you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Ran the [station] on [number]-cover [nights] at a [number]-seat [format] restaurant."
- "Held ticket times under [number] minutes during peak service."
- "Prepped [amount] of [items] per shift to the chef's specs."
- "Cross-trained on [stations]; covered any station on the line."
- "Trained [number] new cooks on [station] and kitchen safety."
- "Kept temperature logs and labeling for [station] through [number] health inspections."
