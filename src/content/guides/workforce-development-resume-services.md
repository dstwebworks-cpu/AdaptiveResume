Every workforce development board delivers resume assistance today. It is delivered as a career service under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), inside job search and placement assistance, and it usually happens in a weekly workshop, in an American Job Center's computer lab, and in whatever hours a case manager can spare. The result is uneven: a participant who came on workshop day gets an hour with a coach, and one who came on Thursday gets a template and a chair.

This guide is about buying that service per participant instead, the way boards already buy skills-training seats and licenses. It covers what a per-participant resume service must include, how it fits the rules boards work under, and what it is worth against the government's own published rates for the same deliverable.

## Where resume assistance sits today

Under the WIOA regulations, job search and placement assistance is a basic career service every one-stop delivers, and resume help is how much of that assistance happens in practice. A board usually delivers it three ways:

- **Workshops.** A group session on resume basics, on a fixed schedule, with a handout and a template.
- **Staff time.** A case manager or workshop facilitator sits with a participant and edits the resume. This is the highest-quality version and the one there is never enough of.
- **The built-in resume builder** on the state's job-matching website. Participants fill in fields and the platform produces a resume. They produce a resume, and rarely a good one.

None of this is wrong. It is what the budget and the staff hours allow. The problem is that resume quality depends on which of the three a participant happened to get, and the people who most need the staff-time version, dislocated workers with twenty years in one plant, justice-involved participants, and veterans, are the ones for whom a handout does the least.

## What a per-participant service must include

If a board buys resume assistance from an outside vendor per participant, hold the vendor to five requirements. They are the same five we hold ourselves to.

**1. It works from what the participant actually did.** A dislocated worker who ran a production line for 18 years and wrote one line about it has a resume problem that no template fixes. The service has to work backwards through the real work, the equipment, the standards, the people trained, and put words to it.

**2. Every line is confirmed by the participant.** Each suggested line is a question the participant answers. Nothing goes on the resume they did not say yes to. This is what keeps a program honest at volume and what makes the resume the participant's own.

**3. Credentials are asked about, never assumed.** A forklift certification, a CDL, a state license. These are checkable, and a resume that claims one the participant does not hold is worse than one that leaves it off.

**4. It documents the service.** Each participant gets a record of what was proposed, what was confirmed, and when. That record is what a case manager needs for the participant's file and what a board needs to show the service was delivered.

**5. The file reads cleanly to screening software.** Single column, standard headings, no graphics, so the first employer's applicant tracking system does not scramble it.

Here is what that looks like for one participant. This is a sample participant, not a real person.

**Before:** "Shift supervisor at a distribution center, 14 years. Supervised the night shift."

**After:** "Shift supervisor for a 22-person night shift at a regional distribution center: about 1,800 orders a night in Manhattan Associates WMS, 99.6% pick accuracy across the last 2 years, reach-truck certified, and training for 15 new hires on receiving and put-away."

Every item in the second version was already the participant's. The work had become routine, and routine work is invisible to the person doing it. A workshop handout would not have found any of it.

## What it is worth: the government's own rates

Boards do not need a vendor's opinion about what resume assistance is worth per person, because state governments already publish what they pay for it. State vocational rehabilitation agencies pay approved outside vendors a flat per-person rate for resume and job-readiness deliverables, and in the states that publish their fee schedules those rates run from about $100 per person for a basic resume up to several hundred dollars for job-readiness training. Tennessee, for example, publishes $349 per person for a customized-employment plan that includes a resume and $696 per person for job-readiness training, at rates effective October 2025. Those are government-set prices for a comparable deliverable, and they are an honest reference for any per-participant quote.

Per-participant purchasing into boards is also an established motion. At least one skills-training platform is licensed by more than a hundred boards on a per-individual basis at under $100 per person. A per-participant resume service fits the same line item.

## Fitting the rules

**Cost reasonableness.** Federal cost principles (2 CFR 200.403 to 200.405) require that costs charged to a WIOA grant be reasonable and allocable. A per-participant rate that sits inside the published state rates for the same deliverable is easy to document as reasonable.

**Procurement.** Many boards can buy a small, fixed-scope pilot below the micro-purchase threshold that applies to their funding stream without a formal solicitation. Which threshold applies depends on the funding source and on state and local procurement policy, so confirm it with your procurement office before you size a pilot. A per-participant price keeps the pilot total small and predictable.

**Performance.** No WIOA performance indicator measures resume quality, and we will not claim one does. We do not claim an effect on the employment-rate indicators either. What a consistent resume service gives the board is staff hours returned to case management and a per-participant service record for the participant's file.

**Data.** Participant records are protected, and any vendor should work under a signed data-processing agreement. During a pilot we handle intake for you under one.

## Format notes

Two pages is normal once a participant has more than one substantive role, and an 18-year employee should not be forced onto one. Reverse chronological order, a single column, no photos or graphics. Gaps get one plain dated line, never an explanation paragraph. For participants pursuing federal jobs, applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages as of the September 2025 Office of Personnel Management (OPM) change. Career services are defined in the WIOA regulations at [20 CFR 678.430](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/chapter-V/part-678/subpart-B/section-678.430).
