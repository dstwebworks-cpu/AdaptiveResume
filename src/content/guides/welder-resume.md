Welding is one of the few trades where a resume can be checked against a plate in about ten minutes — most shops give a weld test before they hire. That doesn't make the resume less important. It decides whether you get called in for the test at all, and it tells the shop which test to set up. A resume that just says "certified welder, 8 years experience" makes both of those harder than they need to be.

The thing that carries the most weight is specificity. Welding certifications are not general badges — a certification names a **process**, a **position**, a **material**, and a thickness range, and it's tied to a Welding Procedure Specification or a code. A welder who knows this and writes it out reads as someone who has actually been through qualification. A welder who leaves it vague reads, fairly or not, like someone who hasn't.

## Lead with your processes and positions

Under each job, give the shop the picture in one line before the bullets: what you welded, on which processes, in which positions, on what materials.

**Before:** "Experienced welder responsible for welding and fabrication on various projects."

**After:** "Structural steel fabrication; SMAW and FCAW, plate and structural shapes, carbon steel 1/8\" to 1\"; qualified 3G and 4G; read shop drawings and weld symbols daily."

The second version is scannable in a few seconds and answers what the first leaves a hiring welder or shop foreman guessing at. It uses the exact terms shops write into their own postings — process abbreviations, position designations, material — so it matches how the job was described in the first place.

## Processes, stated by how you actually run them

The four processes shops screen for most are worth naming individually, with the trade nicknames, because both show up in postings:

- **SMAW (stick)** — the code and field-repair workhorse. Common on structural, pipe, and heavy repair.
- **GMAW (MIG)** — high-deposition production welding; short-circuit, spray, and pulse variants if you know them.
- **GTAW (TIG)** — precision work, thin material, stainless and aluminum, pipe roots.
- **FCAW (flux-cored)** — self-shielded and gas-shielded; heavy structural and out-of-position production.

Say which of these you can be productive on, and be honest about depth. There is a real difference between running production MIG every shift and having tried TIG in school. If a process is limited, write it plainly — "SMAW and FCAW production; GTAW basic." The weld test finds the gap fast, so setting the expectation on the page costs you nothing and builds trust.

## Positions and materials — use the code language

**Positions** have standard designations, and using them signals you've been qualified rather than just "welded a lot." Plate groove positions are **1G (flat), 2G (horizontal), 3G (vertical), and 4G (overhead)**. Pipe adds **5G** (fixed horizontal pipe) and **6G** (fixed 45-degree pipe) — 6G is the demanding all-position pipe test, and if you've passed it, it belongs high on the page. Say which positions you're qualified in and on which process, because qualification is position- and process-specific: "6G pipe, GTAW root with SMAW fill and cap" tells a pipe shop exactly what you can do.

**Materials** matter as much as process. Carbon steel, stainless, and aluminum weld differently and shops hire for the ones they run. Name what you've actually welded, and add thickness range where you know it — thin-gauge sheet and one-inch structural plate are different jobs.

## Certifications — specific, with real status

This is where honesty matters most, and where the detail helps you rather than hurting you. A welder certification is not one thing. The **AWS Certified Welder** program, run by the American Welding Society, tests welders to real production procedures. A qualification under a code like **AWS D1.1** (Structural Welding Code — Steel) is tied to a specific process, position, material, and thickness range — not a blanket "certified."

There's an honest distinction to draw, and drawing it makes you look like you understand your own trade:

- Being **qualified on a specific WPS** at a given shop means you passed a weld test on that shop's procedure — a real, valuable thing, but tied to that procedure.
- Holding a **credential** like AWS Certified Welder or a qualification to a published code is broader, but still names a process, position, and material.

So write the detail. "AWS Certified Welder" alone is fine, but "AWS Certified Welder — SMAW, 3G/4G, carbon steel" is stronger and truer. If you were qualified on an employer's WPS rather than an external credential, say that: "qualified on company WPS, GMAW, 2G/3G, [material]." Many welder qualifications lapse if you go a period without welding on the process, so note current status where it applies rather than implying a cert is permanent.

## Reading, inspection, and the skills shops assume

The parts of the job beyond the arc are worth their own lines, because they separate a welder from someone who only lays bead:

- **Blueprint and weld-symbol reading** — following shop drawings, interpreting weld symbols, and welding to spec. Shops assume it and are relieved to see it stated.
- **Fit-up and prep** — joint preparation, bevels, tacking, and fixturing. On many jobs this is half the work.
- **Visual inspection (VT)** — checking your own welds against acceptance criteria. If you have awareness of nondestructive testing — RT (radiographic), UT (ultrasonic), MT (magnetic particle), or PT (dye penetrant) — say "familiar with," not "perform," unless you actually hold the inspection credential.

If you hold an inspector credential such as **AWS CWI (Certified Welding Inspector)**, that's a separate and significant line — list it explicitly rather than folding it into general "inspection experience."

## Format notes

**Trades resumes are usually one page, and that's genuinely fine here** — but the certification and position detail is what earns the space, so don't cut it to hit a page count. If you've held several roles across structural, pipe, and fabrication, a tight two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantial job to describe. What matters is that every line earns its place. A clear certifications block, a processes-and-positions summary, and real work history beat a padded page of duties every welder shares.

One exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change. That's a hard requirement, not a style preference.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, a clearly labeled certifications block, and a short processes/positions summary near the top. Shops read that summary as a proxy for how fast you'll be productive. Skip the graphics and photos — the plate test is the demo.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Welded [SMAW/GMAW/GTAW/FCAW] on [carbon steel / stainless / aluminum], [thickness range]"
- "Qualified [1G/2G/3G/4G] plate and [5G/6G] pipe on [process]"
- "AWS Certified Welder — [process], [position], [material]" (or "qualified on company WPS — [process], [position]")
- "Read shop drawings and weld symbols; welded to spec on [structural / pipe / pressure] work"
- "Performed fit-up, bevels, and tacking on [assemblies / weldments]"
- "Passed [N]% of radiographed welds on [pipe / structural] jobs" (only if you know the number)
- "Ran [MIG / flux-cored] production at [deposition / footage] on [product]"
- "Performed visual inspection to [AWS D1.1 / project] acceptance criteria; familiar with [RT/UT/MT/PT]"

The highest-risk lines are the ones that overstate scope — a position you weren't qualified in, a process you barely ran, or a cert stated as permanent when it's lapsed. In welding this gets found in one weld test, so it's the wrong place to stretch. Accurate detail is stronger than inflation because it survives the plate.
