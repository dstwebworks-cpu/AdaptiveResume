Paralegal hiring is more segmented than almost any other role at the same pay band. A litigation paralegal who lives in discovery deadlines and a corporate paralegal who manages entity filings are doing different jobs with different evidence, and a resume that reads as generically "legal" competes badly against both. The single most useful thing you can do is make your **practice area** unmissable in the first three lines.

The second most useful thing is putting real numbers on your case work. Legal resumes are unusually vague about volume — plenty say "assisted attorneys with case management" and never say how many cases, in what court, at what stage. Volume and forum are exactly what a hiring attorney is trying to figure out.

## Lead with practice area and forum

Under each employer, give the context in one line before the bullets — practice area, matter type, caseload, and the courts you filed in.

**Before:** "Assisted attorneys with case preparation, filing, and client communication."

**After:** "Plaintiff-side personal injury; ~60 active files at any time, pre-litigation through settlement; filings in Georgia state and superior courts and the Northern District of Georgia via CM/ECF."

The second version is scannable in four seconds and answers what the first version leaves a reader guessing at. It also has a practical local benefit: naming the actual courts and e-filing systems you've used — Odyssey eFileGA, PeachCourt, CM/ECF, or whatever your jurisdiction runs — matches how firms describe their own needs in postings.

## The parts of the job that carry the most weight

**Discovery** is the center of most litigation paralegal resumes. Write the scope: written discovery drafted and responded to, document review volume, privilege logs, subpoenas, records collection. If you've run e-discovery, name the platform — Relativity, Everlaw, Logikcull, Nextpoint — because those are hard filters in larger firms.

**Court filings and calendaring** deserve their own lines. Deadline management under a scheduling order is the responsibility that firms most fear getting wrong, so demonstrated reliability there is genuinely valuable. Name your case management system: Clio, MyCase, Filevine, Smokeball, ProLaw, or iManage.

**Drafting** is where honesty matters most. Say exactly what you drafted and understand the line you're describing: pleadings, motions, discovery responses, correspondence, settlement demands, closing binders, and entity documents are paralegal work. Legal *advice* is not, and a resume that blurs into it makes a careful reader nervous rather than impressed.

**Depositions, mediations, and trial support** — summaries prepared, exhibits assembled, witness coordination, trial notebooks. If you've been onsite for trial, say which role you played.

**Client contact** is real skill in personal injury, family, and immigration work especially. Intake volume, status communication, and records chasing are legitimate lines, and in high-volume practices they're most of the job.

## Practice-area notes

**Litigation:** deadlines, discovery, and forum are your resume. Filing volume and court familiarity are the shorthand a hiring attorney reads for competence.

**Corporate and transactional:** entity formations and annual filings, registered-agent and Secretary of State work, closing checklists, cap-table and minute-book maintenance, due-diligence support. Volume and deal size matter more than adjectives.

**Family law:** high emotional-labor caseloads, financial affidavits, discovery, and hearing prep. Naming caseload honestly is not a weakness — family practices know what their volume feels like.

**Immigration:** form families by name (I-130, I-485, I-589, H-1B, and so on), USCIS filing volume, RFE responses, and language capability if you have it. This is one of the few paralegal specialties where a second language is a direct qualification, so put it where it can be seen.

**Real estate:** title review, closing coordination, settlement statements, recording, and lender requirements.

## Credentials, exactly as held

List your education and any credential with its true status: a paralegal certificate and the granting institution, an associate or bachelor's degree, and NALA's Certified Paralegal or NFPA's Registered Paralegal if you hold them. Notary commission is worth a line — it's small, and firms use it constantly. If you're enrolled in a program, write it as in progress with the expected completion date.

Georgia does not license paralegals (no state does, though California sets rules for who may use the title), so no one can require a credential you don't have — but a firm that lists one in a posting will screen for it. State what's true and let the case work do the arguing.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** The "keep it to one page" advice gets repeated far past the point where it helps. If you've held three paralegal roles across two practice areas, or you managed people somewhere before this career, or you came in from another field that gives you an edge — that's the material that puts you ahead of the other candidates, and cramming it into one page to satisfy a convention throws away your advantage.

The honest rule: **two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive role to describe.** What matters is that every line earns its space. A tight two pages beats a padded one page, and it also beats a two-page resume where the second page is filler. Cut duties everyone in the role shares; keep volume, forum, systems, and anything that shows judgment.

One genuine exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change. That's a hard requirement rather than a style preference.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, practice-area line under every employer, and a clearly labeled software block — legal hiring reads software as a proxy for how quickly you'll be productive. Conservative formatting is genuinely the right call in this field. Skip the graphics.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Managed ~[N] active [practice area] files from [stage] through [stage]"
- "Drafted and responded to written discovery in [matter type]; [N] sets per [month/quarter]"
- "E-filed in [courts] via [CM/ECF / Odyssey eFileGA / PeachCourt]; [N] filings, no rejections"
- "Reviewed and coded [N] documents in [Relativity/Everlaw]; built privilege log of [N] entries"
- "Summarized [N] depositions and prepared exhibit sets for [hearings/trials]"
- "Maintained calendar and deadlines under scheduling orders across [N] matters in [Clio/Filevine]"
- "Prepared [N] [I-130/I-485/entity] filings; drafted [N] RFE responses"
- "Coordinated [N] real estate closings; prepared settlement statements and recorded deeds"

## What screening software looks for on a paralegal resume

Filters commonly read for: litigation support, discovery, e-discovery, document review, privilege log, pleadings, motions, subpoenas, deposition summaries, trial preparation, e-filing, CM/ECF, case management, calendaring, docketing, legal research, Westlaw, LexisNexis, Relativity, Clio, entity formation, due diligence, and closing binders. [O*NET's paralegal profile](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/23-2011.00) has the full task list.

The highest-risk lines are the ones that overstate scope — a title you didn't hold, drafting you didn't do, or court experience you observed rather than performed. Legal employers check, and the profession is small enough that they check informally too.
