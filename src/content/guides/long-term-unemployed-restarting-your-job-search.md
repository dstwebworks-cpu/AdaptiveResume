If you have been looking for work for six months or more, you are far from alone. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics counts people jobless for 27 weeks or more as long-term unemployed, and in its [September 2026 report](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm) that was 1.9 million people, 27.1 percent of everyone who was unemployed.

A long search changes a few practical things, and it opens doors to some free help many people never hear about. This guide covers both, along with how to keep your skills current and how to handle the gap on your resume. It is general information, so check the details with your state and your local American Job Center.

## What changes after six months

**Unemployment benefits may be ending.** The Department of Labor's [unemployment insurance fact sheet](https://oui.doleta.gov/unemploy/uifactsheet.asp) says regular benefits can be paid for a maximum of 26 weeks in most states, and that additional weeks may be available during times of high unemployment. Your state's unemployment agency can tell you exactly when your claim ends and whether any extra weeks apply. If you need your state's site, our [list of official unemployment sites for every state](/guides/how-to-file-for-unemployment-by-state/) links each one.

**Your health coverage options may have changed.** If your income has dropped, check Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program. [HealthCare.gov](https://www.healthcare.gov/medicaid-chip/) says they provide free or low-cost coverage to eligible low-income adults, families and children, and that you can apply any time of year. It also says to apply even if you don't think you will qualify based on your income. HealthCare.gov notes that starting 01/01/2027 some states may add a monthly work requirement, so check your state's rules when you apply.

**Some hiring managers read a long gap differently.** This is worth knowing, not worth fearing. A [field experiment by Kroft, Lange and Notowidigdo](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18387), published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, found that the chance of getting a callback for an interview went down as unemployment went on, with most of that drop in the first eight months. The practical lesson is to give a hiring manager something recent to read: current training, recent work of any kind, and a calm, honest line about the time away.

## The free help built for this: the WIOA Dislocated Worker program

The Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) funds a Dislocated Worker program, and many people who were laid off qualify for it.

**Who counts as a dislocated worker.** Federal law ([29 U.S.C. 3102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/3102)) generally includes someone who:

- was laid off or terminated, or received a notice of layoff;
- is eligible for unemployment compensation or has already used it up (or has enough work history but wasn't eligible for it); and
- is unlikely to return to their previous industry or occupation.

The same definition also covers people laid off in a plant or facility closure, self-employed people (including farmers, ranchers and fishermen) who are out of work because of local economic conditions or natural disasters, displaced homemakers, and certain spouses of active-duty service members who lost a job because of a move to a new duty station. Notice the words "has already used it up": running out of unemployment benefits does not, by itself, shut you out.

**What it offers.** The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/workforce-investment/adult) says the Dislocated Worker program is designed to help workers get back to work as quickly as possible, and that its services are provided through the national network of American Job Centers. The companion Adult program provides job search assistance and training opportunities.

**How training is paid for.** Training usually runs through an Individual Training Account. The federal regulation ([20 CFR 680.300](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/20/680.300)) describes it as a payment agreement set up on your behalf with a training provider, and says participants choose from the state's eligible training providers in consultation with a career planner. In plain terms: if you qualify, the program can pay an approved school or training provider directly for a course you pick with your counselor's help.

**How to start.** Visit or call your local American Job Center and ask about the WIOA Dislocated Worker program. Use the [American Job Center Finder](https://www.careeronestop.org/LocalHelp/AmericanJobCenters/find-american-job-centers.aspx) or call 1-877-US-2JOBS. Bring your layoff paperwork and your unemployment claim information, and ask what the center needs to decide whether you qualify.

## Keep your skills current and on the record

A recent date on your resume helps answer the question a hiring manager is already asking: what have you been doing lately? Any of these counts, as long as you describe it honestly:

- **A course or certification.** A training program through the Dislocated Worker program, a community college class, or an industry certification gives you something current to list. Our guide on [how to list certifications on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume/) shows where they go.
- **Contract, temporary or part-time work.** Short assignments are real work with real dates. If you did freelance or self-employed work, our guide on [how to list self-employment on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-self-employment-on-resume/) shows how to present it.
- **Volunteer work that uses your skills.** Keeping the books for a nonprofit or running logistics for a community event is work a hiring manager can picture.

Pick what fits your field and your budget. The point is a recent, true entry, not a busy one.

## Go back to the people who know your work

After months of online applications, the people who have already seen you work are often the most useful part of a search. Former coworkers, supervisors, customers and vendors know what you did and how you did it, and they hear about openings before they are posted. A short, specific note works better than a general request for help: say what kind of role you are looking for, mention a project you worked on together, and ask whether they know anyone you should talk to.

Ask a few of them whether they would be willing to serve as a reference, and tell them which jobs you are applying for so they know what to speak to. If you belonged to a trade association, a union or a professional group, its job board and local meetings are worth a fresh look too. None of this replaces applying, but it puts your name in front of people who can vouch for it.

## Write the gap honestly

A gap of six months or more is common, and you don't need to hide it. Our guide on [how to explain an employment gap on your resume](/guides/how-to-explain-employment-gap-on-resume/) walks through honest ways to handle it, from a one-line note on the resume to a calm answer in an interview. Never change dates to shrink a gap; an explained gap is a detail, and a discovered cover-up can end the process.
