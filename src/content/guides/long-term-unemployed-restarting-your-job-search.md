If you have been looking for work for six months or more, you are far from alone. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics counts people jobless for 27 weeks or more as long-term unemployed, and in its [September 2026 report](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm) that was 1.9 million people, 27.1 percent of everyone who was unemployed.

A long search changes a few practical things, and now is a good time to ask about public help. This guide is general information, so check the details with your state and your local American Job Center.

## What changes after six months

**Unemployment benefits may be ending.** The Department of Labor's [unemployment insurance fact sheet](https://oui.doleta.gov/unemploy/uifactsheet.asp) says regular benefits can be paid for a maximum of 26 weeks in most states, and that additional weeks may be available during times of high unemployment. Your state's unemployment agency can tell you exactly when your claim ends and whether any extra weeks apply. If you need your state's site, our [list of official unemployment sites for every state](/guides/how-to-file-for-unemployment-by-state/) links each one.

**Your health coverage options may have changed.** If your income has dropped, check Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program. [HealthCare.gov](https://www.healthcare.gov/medicaid-chip/) says they provide free or low-cost coverage to eligible low-income adults, families and children, and that you can apply any time of year. It also says to apply even if you don't think you will qualify based on your income. HealthCare.gov notes that starting 01/01/2027, in states that cover adults based on income alone, you may need to meet a monthly work requirement to get or keep Medicaid, so check your state's rules when you apply.

**A long gap can mean fewer callbacks from hiring managers.** A 2012 [field experiment by Kroft, Lange and Notowidigdo](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18387), published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, found that the chance of getting a callback for an interview went down as unemployment went on, with most of that drop in the first eight months. Our practical takeaway is to give a hiring manager something recent to read: current training, recent work of any kind, and a calm, honest line about the time away.

## The help built for this: the WIOA Dislocated Worker program

The Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) funds a Dislocated Worker program, and many people who were laid off may qualify for it.

**Who counts as a dislocated worker.** Federal law ([29 U.S.C. 3102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/3102)) generally includes someone who:

- was laid off or terminated, or received a notice of layoff;
- is eligible for unemployment compensation or has already used it up (or has enough work history but wasn't eligible for it); and
- is unlikely to return to their previous industry or occupation.

So running out of benefits does not, by itself, shut you out of the program.

The definition also covers:

- people laid off in a permanent closure or a substantial layoff at their plant, facility or company;
- self-employed people (including farmers, ranchers and fishermen) who are out of work because of local economic conditions or natural disasters;
- people who stayed home caring for their family and lost the family income they depended on (the law calls them displaced homemakers); and
- certain spouses of active-duty service members who lost a job because of a move to a new duty station.

**What it offers.** The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/workforce-investment/adult) says the Dislocated Worker program is designed to help workers get back to work as quickly as possible, and that its services are offered through American Job Centers. The same Department of Labor page also describes the WIOA Adult program, which provides job search help and training, so ask the center about both.

**How training is paid for.** Training usually runs through an Individual Training Account, which the federal regulation ([20 CFR 680.300](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/20/680.300)) describes as a payment agreement set up on your behalf with a training provider. If you qualify and training is approved for you, you choose a school or provider from your state's eligible list with help from a career planner, and the program can pay that school or provider through the account.

**How to start.** Visit or call your local American Job Center and ask about the WIOA Dislocated Worker program. To find your nearest center, use the [American Job Center Finder](https://www.careeronestop.org/LocalHelp/AmericanJobCenters/find-american-job-centers.aspx) or call 1-877-US-2JOBS. When you contact the center, ask what it needs from you to decide whether you qualify.

## Keep your skills current and on the record

A recent entry on your resume shows a hiring manager what you have been doing lately. Any of these counts, as long as you describe it honestly:

- **A course or certification.** Training paid for by the Dislocated Worker program, a community college class, or an industry certification gives you something current to list. Our guide on [how to list certifications on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume/) shows where they go.
- **Contract, temporary or part-time work.** Short assignments are real work with real dates. If you did freelance or self-employed work, our guide on [how to list self-employment on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-self-employment-on-resume/) shows how to present it.
- **Volunteer work that uses your skills.** Keeping the books for a nonprofit or running logistics for a community event is work a hiring manager can picture.

Pick what fits your field and your budget. The point is to add something recent and true, not to look busy.

## Check your agreement, then go back to the people who know your work

Before you reach out to former coworkers or supervisors who still work at the company, read any severance or employment agreement you signed, because it may limit who you can contact and what you can say about the company. Use only contact details you have on your own, never ones from the company's systems. Former coworkers and supervisors have seen you work and know what you did. Keep your note short and specific: say what kind of role you are looking for, mention a project you worked on together, and ask whether they know anyone you should talk to.

Ask a few of them whether they would be willing to serve as a reference, and tell them which jobs you are applying for so they know what to speak to. If you belonged to a trade association, a union or a professional group, check whether it has a job board or local meetings. None of this replaces applying, but it puts your name in front of people who can vouch for you.

## Write the gap honestly

A gap of six months or more is common, and you don't need to hide it. Our guide on [how to explain an employment gap on your resume](/guides/how-to-explain-employment-gap-on-resume/) walks through honest ways to show it. Never change your dates to make a gap look shorter. Show the real dates, and explain the gap in one calm, honest line.
