A job search turns into a lot of moving parts fast: dozens of applications, a handful of people to follow up with, and dates that are easy to lose track of. A simple tracker keeps it all in one place, so you always know what you sent, to whom, and what happens next. If you are receiving unemployment benefits, it also helps you keep the job-search record your state may ask for.

You can download the Excel job search tracker from the box near the end of this page. It covers your applications, contacts and follow-ups. Its companion, the printable weekly claim and work-search log, is free on our [page about filing for unemployment](/guides/how-to-file-for-unemployment-by-state/), with a row for each weekly claim and a row for each employer you contact.

## What's in the free job search tracker

The tracker is a Microsoft Excel workbook with four tabs.

- **How to use.** This tab has short instructions and a note about unemployment work-search records.
- **Applications.** This tab has one row per job, with columns for the date you applied, the company, the job title, where you found it, a link to the posting, the resume version you sent, your contact's name and email or phone, the status, the date of the last update, your next step, a follow-up date, and notes.
- **Contacts.** This tab is for everyone you talk to during the search, such as recruiters, hiring managers and people who refer you, with the date you last spoke and the date to reach out next.
- **Summary.** This tab fills in its counts on its own: how many applications you are tracking, how many you sent in the last seven days, how many follow-ups are due, how many contacts are due, and how many applications sit at each status.

Two things make it more than a list. First, the Status column has a set list to choose from: saved, applied, heard back, interview scheduled, interviewed, offer, not selected, withdrew and no response. That keeps the Summary tab's counts accurate. Second, when a follow-up date passes and the job is still open, the whole row turns amber, so a follow-up you meant to send doesn't slip by. The same thing happens on the Contacts tab when a reach-out date passes.

The first row on the Applications and Contacts tabs is an example, so you can see how a filled-in row looks. Replace it with your own information and type in the white cells.

## How to get the most out of a tracker

A tracker only helps if it's current, and a few habits keep it that way.

**Log each application the same day.** It takes a minute when the details are in front of you and much longer a week later, when three postings for similar jobs have blurred together.

**Save a copy of the job description.** Paste the link into the Posting link column, and also save the text of the posting somewhere you can find it. A posting can come down before you hear back, and the description is what you'll want to reread before an interview.

**Write down the resume version you sent.** If you change your resume for different kinds of jobs, name each version and record which one went where. When a recruiter calls about a job you applied to three weeks ago, you'll know exactly which page they are looking at.

**Set a follow-up date for every application.** You decide the timing; the tracker makes sure you don't forget it. When the row turns amber, either follow up or update the status.

**Look at the Summary tab once a week.** The counts show how your search is going in a way your memory can't. A month of steady applications with few replies is useful information. It's a reason to look again at your resume and at the kinds of jobs you are applying for, not a verdict on you.

## Write down every interview the same day

Details from an interview slip away fastest, and they are the ones you need most when a second round or an offer comes. On the day of each interview, add a short note to that job's row:

- the names and titles of the people you met, spelled the way they wrote them;
- the date, and whether it was by phone, video or in person;
- what they asked about most, and which parts of your work they wanted to hear more about;
- anything you said you would send, such as references or a work sample, and the date you sent it;
- what they said about next steps and when you should hear back.

Then set the follow-up date to match what they told you, and move the status to "Interviewed." If a recruiter calls weeks later, the row tells you who you met and what you talked about, so you can pick up the conversation where it left off.

## Keeping a work-search log for unemployment

The U.S. Department of Labor's [unemployment insurance fact sheet](https://oui.doleta.gov/unemploy/uifactsheet.asp) says that after your first claim, you must file weekly or biweekly claims and answer questions about whether you are still eligible. Your state may also ask you to record the employers you contact while you look for work. Each state sets its own rules about what counts as a contact, how many it wants, and how you report them, so read your state's instructions when you file. Our [list of official unemployment sites for every state](/guides/how-to-file-for-unemployment-by-state/) links each state's page.

A few practical points:

- **Write each contact down the day you make it.** Record the date, the employer, the job title, how you applied or reached out, and the person or website.
- **Use your state's format if it has one.** Some states want contacts reported in their own online system or on their own form. Our log and tracker are personal records, not official state forms.
- **Keep your records.** Hold on to your log and any confirmation numbers in case your state asks about a past week.

The printable weekly log has two parts: a weekly claims table (week ending, date you filed, confirmation number) and a job-search contacts table. The Excel tracker's Applications tab holds most of the same details if you would rather keep everything on a computer; put who you contacted in the Contact name column and how you applied in the Notes column.

## When your tracker tells you something

After a few weeks, the tracker shows you patterns. If you're sending applications and hearing very little back, two things are worth checking before you send more: whether your resume reads cleanly to screening software, and whether it shows hiring managers the skills the posting asks for.
