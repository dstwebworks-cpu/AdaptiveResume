Aircraft maintenance hiring runs on one question before any other: what are you certificated to sign off? A resume that leads with "10 years of aircraft maintenance experience" and buries the A&P three lines into the education section is answering the second question before the first. The certificate is the gate, and it belongs where it can't be missed.

The second thing a maintenance manager reads for is what you actually worked on — the operation you worked under, the aircraft types, and whether you did return-to-service work or handed it up the chain. Generic maintenance resumes lose here because they describe tasks everyone in the shop shares and skip the specifics that separate a line mechanic from someone who signs the logbook.

## Lead with the certificate, then the work

Put your certificate status in the first lines, stated exactly, then give the context of each job before the bullets — the operation, the aircraft, and your scope.

**Before:** "Experienced aircraft mechanic responsible for maintenance, repairs, and inspections on various aircraft."

**After:** "A&P certificated (Airframe & Powerplant, 14 CFR Part 65); IA held. Part 145 repair station line and heavy maintenance on [Boeing 737NG] and [CRJ700]; performed and signed return-to-service entries, AD compliance research, and [C-check] tasks."

![Before and after example of an improved resume line - every line confirmed by the person](/img/guides/examples/aircraft-mechanic-resume-example.png)

The second version answers in four seconds what the first leaves a reader guessing: what you're certificated for, what environment you worked in, which aircraft, and whether you were trusted to return them to service. Naming the operating rule — Part 145, Part 121, or Part 135 — matters because a hiring shop describes its own work that way, and it tells them how your paperwork habits and oversight will translate.

## State your certificate exactly as the FAA issued it

Your FAA mechanic certificate carries an **Airframe** rating, a **Powerplant** rating, or both. "A&P" means you hold both. If you hold only one, say so — "Airframe rating held; Powerplant in progress" is honest and still valuable. The certificate is issued under **14 CFR Part 65**, which is worth naming once because it signals you know the framework rather than the slang.

If you hold an **Inspection Authorization (IA)**, list it plainly. Eligibility for the IA is set under **14 CFR §65.91**, and its privileges under **§65.95** — the holder can inspect and approve major repairs and major alterations for return to service, and perform annual inspections. You must have held both ratings for at least three years, and been actively maintaining aircraft for at least the two years before you apply, just to qualify to test for it; the authorization then **expires on March 31 of each odd-numbered year** — so state that it's current.

Be careful with the **avionics** line. Avionics technicians work on electrical, navigation, and communication systems, and many hold an FCC license or manufacturer certifications rather than an A&P. If your work is avionics, describe it as avionics; if it's airframe and powerplant, keep it there. Blurring the two reads as either padding or confusion to someone who lives in these distinctions.

If your background is military — whatever your rating or specialty code called it, aviation maintenance technician (**AMT**) work is the same trade under a different banner — translate the airframes, engines, and maintenance level into civilian terms, and be straight about whether you've converted your experience into an FAA certificate yet.

## Show return-to-service and airworthiness work

The line between a mechanic and a *signing* mechanic is return-to-service authority, and your resume should make clear which side you were on.

**Logbook and return-to-service entries.** If you made and signed maintenance record entries and returned aircraft to service, that's a high-trust responsibility — write it. If you performed the work and a certificated mechanic signed it off, describe your work accurately without claiming the signature.

**Airworthiness Directive (AD) compliance.** Researching applicable ADs, performing the required action, and documenting compliance is exactly the paperwork discipline a maintenance manager is trying to hire for. Name it. The same goes for service bulletins and manufacturer maintenance manual tasks.

**Inspections.** Say which ones: annual, 100-hour, progressive, phase checks, or heavy-check inputs (A-check, C-check). If you hold an IA and performed annuals, that's your headline.

**Troubleshooting and run-up.** Diagnosing a squawk, running an engine, and clearing a discrepancy shows you can find the fault, not just swap the part on the card. Borescope inspection of engines belongs here too.

## Name the systems and materials you actually worked

Maintenance is broad, and the specifics are what get you screened in. Keep only what's true:

- **Airframe and structures:** sheet metal repair, riveting, corrosion control, composites (repair and inspection), flight controls, landing gear.
- **Powerplant:** reciprocating and turbine engines, engine change, run-up and troubleshooting, borescope inspection, propeller and rotor work if applicable.
- **Systems:** hydraulics, pneumatics, fuel, electrical, environmental/pressurization.
- **Aircraft types worked:** name them. "737NG," "A320 family," "King Air," "CRJ," "Sikorsky S-76" — type experience is one of the hardest filters in this field, and it's the fastest way for a shop to picture you on their floor.

Only claim inspection methods you're qualified in. Borescope is common line work. Formal **non-destructive testing (NDT)** — eddy current, ultrasonic, dye penetrant, magnetic particle — is often a separate qualification, so list an NDT method only if you're actually certified or trained in it.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** The old "one page" advice wastes the very experience that makes you hireable. If you've worked heavy maintenance and line, held roles at a repair station and an airline, or carried a lead or crew-chief role, that's the material that puts you ahead — don't crush it to fit a convention.

The honest rule: **two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive role to describe.** Every line still has to earn its space. Cut the generic duties every mechanic shares; keep certificates, aircraft types, return-to-service scope, and anything that shows judgment.

One real exception: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change — a hard requirement, not a style preference. That matters for FAA, DoD, and depot-level roles.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, a clearly labeled certificates block up top, and a systems/aircraft-types block that reads at a glance. Conservative formatting is the right call — skip the graphics and columns that trip up applicant tracking systems.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Held FAA A&P certificate (Airframe & Powerplant, 14 CFR Part 65); [IA held, renews [MM/YYYY]]"
- "Performed and signed return-to-service entries on [aircraft type] under Part [145/121/135]"
- "Researched and documented AD compliance on [N] aircraft; [zero] repeat discrepancies"
- "Completed [annual / 100-hour / phase / C-check] inspections on [aircraft type]"
- "Troubleshot and cleared [system] squawks; performed engine run-up and borescope inspection"
- "Executed sheet metal / composite repairs per [manufacturer maintenance manual / SRM]"
- "Removed and installed [turbine/reciprocating] engine; [rigged flight controls / serviced landing gear]"
- "Maintained maintenance records in [logbook system / CAMP / TRAX]; [no audit findings over N years]"

Aviation is a documented, audited field and employers verify certificates against FAA records. Accuracy isn't just honest here; it's the thing that keeps you hireable.
