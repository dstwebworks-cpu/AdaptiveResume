Three questions belong near the top of a speech language pathologist resume. Do you hold your CCC-SLP (Certificate of Clinical Competence in Speech-Language Pathology) and a current state license? Which setting do you know? And which disorders and ages have you really treated? A resume that says "evaluated and treated patients with communication disorders" could describe every person in the profession.

Speech language pathology splits sharply between schools and medical settings. A therapist managing an elementary caseload and one running swallow studies in a hospital use different tests, different rules, and different vocabulary. Here is how to show your own practice clearly to a hiring manager and to screening software.

## The practice line: your work in one sentence

Put one line under each job that tells a hiring manager what you carried.

**Before:** "Speech language pathologist responsible for evaluating and treating patients with communication and swallowing disorders."

**After:** "Speech-language pathologist, CCC-SLP ([state] license, active); [40]-bed acute rehab unit; [9] patients per day across [aphasia, dysarthria, and dysphagia]; completed [modified barium swallow studies]; wrote plans of care; [Epic] documentation."

Write your numbers from memory and from records you own. Do not go back into a former employer's charting or special education system to collect them, and keep every patient and student detail off the page. If you signed a severance or confidentiality agreement, read it before you write any number, because it is a contract and breaking it can put your severance at risk. The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission [explains what it means to give up legal claims in a severance agreement](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/qa-understanding-waivers-discrimination-claims-employee-severance-agreements), and an employment attorney can tell you what yours allows.

## CCC-SLP, your state license, and your fellowship

Three separate things can sit behind your title, and each one needs its own accurate line.

**The CCC-SLP** comes from ASHA (the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association). The path runs through a graduate degree, a passing score on the Praxis exam in speech-language pathology, and a clinical fellowship. ASHA requires the fellowship to run at least 36 weeks and 1,260 hours.

**A state license** comes from your state board, and it has its own number, status, and renewal date. Write it as your board shows it: "[State] speech-language pathology license #[N], active." Requirements differ from state to state, including for people still in their fellowship, so write the exact type of license you hold.

**A school credential** is a separate item in some states, where the education agency issues it. If you hold one, list it by its real name. If you work in schools under your state license alone, you do not need to add anything.

List a privilege under the Audiology and Speech-Language Pathology Interstate Compact only if you actually hold one.

Speech-language pathology assistants carry out treatment under a speech-language pathologist's (SLP's) supervision and have a different credential path. If that is your job, write your own title and credential, and do not use "SLP" alone.

## Credentials, exactly as held

Our guide on [how to list certifications on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume/) covers where they go. Beyond the CCC-SLP and your license, list only what you hold.

- **BCS-S.** The Board Certified Specialist in Swallowing and Swallowing Disorders credential comes from the American Board of Swallowing and Swallowing Disorders. Use the initials only if the board has awarded them to you.
- **Treatment program certificates.** Programs such as LSVT LOUD (Lee Silverman Voice Treatment LOUD), PROMPT (Prompts for Restructuring Oral Muscular Phonetic Targets), and VitalStim each have their own training. Write the program's name, the level you completed if it has levels, and the date your certification runs through if it renews, and describe a course you attended as a course.
- **BLS.** If your medical employer asked for it, write the issuer and the date your Basic Life Support card runs through.

## Schools, hospitals, and the settings in between

**Schools.** Give your caseload size, grade levels, and service models, such as pull-out, push-in, or teletherapy. Say how many IEPs (individualized education programs) you wrote or managed under IDEA (the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act), and mention evaluations you led and any Medicaid billing you handled.

**Hospitals and rehab.** Name the units you covered and your work with swallowing, cognition, and aphasia. Instrumental studies carry a lot of weight here, so name them: MBSS (modified barium swallow study) or FEES (fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing). Say whether you performed them on your own.

**Skilled nursing and home health.** Give your caseload and your dysphagia and cognitive work, and say which payer rules you documented under, such as Medicare Part A or Part B.

**Pediatric clinics and early intervention.** Give age ranges, the disorders you treated, feeding work, and how you coached parents. In early intervention, mention IFSPs (individualized family service plans).

**Private practice and teletherapy.** Give your weekly client count and the platform you used. If you ran the practice, include scheduling, billing, and insurance work.

## Your evaluations, your caseload, and your charting

**Evaluations.** Say how many you complete each month and name the tools. School and pediatric therapists may use the CELF-5 (Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals), the GFTA-3 (Goldman-Fristoe Test of Articulation), or the PLS-5 (Preschool Language Scales). Medical therapists may use the WAB-R (Western Aphasia Battery-Revised) or the MBSImP (Modified Barium Swallow Impairment Profile).

**Disorders treated.** Name them: articulation, phonological disorders, language, fluency, voice, aphasia, apraxia, dysarthria, dysphagia, and cognitive-communication disorders. Leave out any area you have not treated in real practice.

**AAC.** Augmentative and alternative communication is its own area of practice. Name the devices and apps you programmed or trained on, such as Tobii Dynavox devices, TouchChat, or Proloquo2Go.

**Teamwork and leadership.** Include clinical fellows you mentored, assistants you supervised, graduate students you hosted, and IEP or rehab teams you worked on.

**Systems.** Name Epic, Oracle Health (formerly Cerner), WebPT, PointClickCare, or your special education platform by its product name, which is what screening software looks for.

## Clinical fellows: your fellowship is a real job

Put your fellowship at the top of your experience and describe it the way you would describe any job. Give the setting, your caseload, and your mentor's credential, and leave out your mentor's name. If your state issued a temporary or provisional license for your fellowship, write it exactly as your board shows it. If you passed the Praxis exam, you can add "Praxis in Speech-Language Pathology, passed [year]."

**Before:** "Clinical fellow providing speech therapy services under supervision."

**After:** "Clinical Fellow, [elementary school]; caseload of [38] students in grades [K-3]; wrote [22] IEPs and led [14] evaluations; mentored by a [CCC-SLP]; fellowship expected to finish [month year]."

## Format notes

**Let your experience decide the length.** One page is right for a clinical fellow or a new graduate. Two pages is normal once you have more than one substantive role to describe. Put your certificate and license near the top in their own labeled section, and start each job with your practice line.

Federal applications through USAJOBS generally cap at two pages as of the September 2025 OPM (Office of Personnel Management) change. The VA (Department of Veterans Affairs) says that limit does not apply to its speech-language pathologist jobs, so follow the resume instructions in the job announcement.

List your most recent job first, in one column with standard headings and real text. Graphics and tables get in the way of screening software, so leave them out.

## Speech-language pathology bullets you can adapt, keeping only what's true

- "Speech-language pathologist, CCC-SLP ([state] license, active); [setting]; [N] patients per day"
- "Managed a school caseload of [N] students in grades [range]; wrote and updated [N] IEPs per year under IDEA"
- "Completed [N] evaluations per month using [CELF-5 / GFTA-3 / PLS-5]"
- "Completed [N] [MBSS / FEES] studies per month and wrote diet and strategy recommendations for the care team"
- "Treated [aphasia / apraxia / dysphagia / fluency] in [setting], with plans of care written to [Medicare Part A / Part B] rules"
- "Programmed and trained families on [AAC device or app] for [N] nonspeaking students"
- "Mentored [N] clinical fellows and supervised [N] speech-language pathology assistants"
- "Delivered teletherapy to [N] clients per week on [platform] across [N] states where licensed"

A bullet earns its place only if you could talk a hiring manager through it.

## What screening software looks for on a speech language pathologist resume

Terms that often appear in speech-language pathology postings include speech-language pathologist, speech language pathologist, SLP, CCC-SLP, state license, clinical fellowship, dysphagia, aphasia, articulation, language disorders, AAC, IEP, MBSS, FEES, evaluation, plan of care, and your settings and systems by name. Use both "speech-language pathologist" and "SLP" somewhere on the page, because postings use both.

[The U.S. Department of Labor's Occupational Information Network, or O*NET, lists this work under Speech-Language Pathologists (29-1127.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/29-1127.00), and its task list can remind you of work you did and left off. Write your job title the way your license shows it.

Be most careful with anything that overstates your status. "CCC-SLP" written during a fellowship and a lapsed license written as active can be checked quickly, and an instrumental study you only observed tends to come out when someone asks you about it.
