Every internal mobility platform makes the same promise: open roles get matched to the skills your people already have, so you fill from inside before you hire from outside, and you redeploy people before you lay them off. The promise depends on one input the platform does not create: an accurate record of what each employee can actually do.

That record is the weak link, and this guide is about it. It covers what the platform category does and does not do, what a redeployment actually requires, why employee skills profiles are thin, and how to build a confirmed skills record per employee, whether you are buying a platform, renewing one, or running a redeployment with a spreadsheet.

## What the platform category does

An internal mobility platform, also sold as an internal talent marketplace, holds two lists and a matching layer. The first list is demand: open roles, projects, stretch assignments, and sometimes short gigs. The second list is supply: employee profiles with skills, experience, and stated interests. The matching layer scores each employee against each opening and surfaces the fit to the employee, the manager, or both.

The category is real and the large vendors are serious products. What none of them do is discover skills that are not in the profile. Most infer skills from a job title and whatever the employee typed in during onboarding. A profile that says "Production Supervisor" gets the skills a production supervisor is assumed to have. It does not get the fact that this particular supervisor also ran the plant's ISO 9001 audit prep for four years, trained every new hire on the lockout procedure, and covered the scheduler's role for a year. Those are the facts that make her a fit for the quality role you are about to post, and the platform has never seen them.

## What redeployment actually requires

Redeployment is the decision to move people whose current roles are ending into roles you still need, instead of separating them and hiring strangers. It is worth doing for the plain reason that a person who already knows your systems, your customers, and your culture is cheaper to move than to replace. It also changes the tone of a restructuring, inside and outside the company.

If the restructuring is large enough, notice rules apply on their own timeline. The federal WARN Act generally requires employers with 100 or more employees to give 60 days' written notice before a plant closing or a mass layoff, and several states set stricter thresholds. A redeployment program does not change those obligations. It runs alongside them, and the notice window is often exactly the time in which the skills record has to be built.

A redeployment program needs three things:

1. **A current record of what each affected employee can do**, in enough detail to match against a role description, and confirmed by the employee so nobody is moved on the strength of a guess.
2. **A list of the roles you need filled** in the next two quarters, including the ones you had planned to hire for externally.
3. **Someone accountable for the match**, with the authority to move people across departments.

The second and third items are management decisions. The first is the work, and it is the part employers underestimate. Nobody's HR profile is current, least of all the profiles of long-tenured people, because the longer you do a job the less of it you write down.

## Why skills profiles are thin

Employees write their own profiles, usually once, at onboarding or when a platform launches. Three things go wrong from there.

**Routine work becomes invisible.** A person who has run the same process for twelve years stops seeing it as a skill. Asked to list their skills, they write the job title.

**The profile records the current role, not the career.** The maintenance technician who was a licensed electrician in a previous job, the customer service lead who ran payroll at a smaller company, the analyst who administered the CRM before the company had an admin. None of that is in a profile that asks about the current job.

**Credentials and training go unrecorded.** Forklift certifications, safety training, software administration, language skills, a state license from a prior career. These are the checkable facts that make a match defensible, and they live in a drawer.

The result is that a platform matching on profiles is matching on the least complete data in the building. Better matching algorithms do not fix thin inputs.

## Building a confirmed skills record per employee

The fix is a skills excavation done per person, before the platform runs, or instead of it for a small redeployment. The method is the same one we use for individual resumes, applied to a batch of employees.

1. **Start from what exists.** The current resume, the HR profile, the job description, and, if the employee chooses, a LinkedIn export. For long-tenured employees the current resume is often years old; that is fine, it is a starting point.
2. **Work backwards through the real work.** The equipment, the systems, the standards, the people trained, the coverage assignments, the projects nobody wrote down. Every likely skill becomes a question.
3. **The employee confirms every line.** Nothing goes on the record the person did not say yes to. This is what makes the record honest at volume, and what makes it the employee's own rather than HR's assumption.
4. **Credentials are asked about, never assumed.** A certification the person no longer holds is worse on a record than one that was never listed.
5. **The output is a finished record.** A finished resume in DOCX and PDF per employee, built only from confirmed lines, a per-employee Review Audit report in PDF showing what was proposed and what was confirmed, and an organization report in CSV with each person's review status. Your team reads the confirmed lines from the resume into a platform profile or a matching spreadsheet; nothing loads automatically.

Here is what the difference looks like for one person. This is a sample employee, not a real one.

**Before, from the HR profile:** "Production Supervisor, 12 years. Supervises second shift."

**After, confirmed line by line:** "Production supervisor for a 34-person second shift on two packaging lines; ran ISO 9001 internal audit preparation for four consecutive audits; trained 60 or more new hires on lockout-tagout under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147; covered the production scheduler role for 11 months during a leave, building the weekly schedule in SAP."

The role you are about to post for a quality coordinator asks for audit preparation experience and a documented training history. The platform would never have surfaced her. The confirmed record does, once someone reads it against the role.

## Buying, renewing, or skipping the platform

**If you are buying a platform,** run the skills excavation first, on the population you most need to move, and copy the confirmed lines into the profiles before launch. A platform that goes live on thin profiles produces weak matches in its first months, and employees stop trusting it.

**If you are renewing one,** ask the vendor how many employees have updated their profile in the last twelve months. That number is the platform's real coverage.

**If you are redeploying fewer than a few hundred people,** you may not need a platform at all. A confirmed skills record per person and a role list will do the matching, and the money goes to the work instead of the license.
