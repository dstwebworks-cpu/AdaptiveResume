Start your occupational therapist resume by answering three questions. Is your license current, and do you hold your NBCOT (National Board for Certification in Occupational Therapy) certification? Which setting and population do you know? And did you carry your own evaluations and plans of care? A resume that says "provided occupational therapy services to improve patients' daily living skills" could describe almost anyone in the field.

Occupational therapy changes completely from one setting to the next. A school therapist writing goals for an IEP (individualized education program) and a hand therapist fitting a custom orthosis do very different work under one title. This guide helps you make your own practice plain, in words a hiring manager and screening software can both find.

## The evaluation line: your practice in one sentence

Put one line under each job that tells a rehab manager what you carried. In the example below, OTR/L means you are an Occupational Therapist Registered (OTR) and hold a state license (the L).

**Before:** "Occupational therapist responsible for evaluating and treating patients to improve activities of daily living."

**After:** "Occupational therapist, OTR/L ([state]); [32]-bed inpatient rehab unit; [10] patients per day and [4] evaluations per week across [stroke and brain injury]; wrote plans of care; activities of daily living (ADL) retraining, upper extremity function, and home safety planning; [Epic] documentation."

Write your numbers from memory and from records you own. Do not go back into a former employer's charting system to collect them, and keep every patient detail off the page. If you signed a severance or confidentiality agreement, read it before you write any number, because it is a contract and breaking it can put your severance at risk. ## OTR, your state license, and what each one means

Two separate things sit behind the letters after your name, and your resume should show each one.

**NBCOT certification** gives you the OTR (Occupational Therapist Registered) credential after you pass the national exam. You renew it on a three-year cycle. **A state license** is what permits you to practice, and every state, along with the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico, requires one. The two run on separate renewals, so check the status of each one before you write it down.

Write both on the page with their real status: "OTR/L, [state] license #[N], active; NBCOT certified through [year]." If you hold licenses in more than one state, list each one. If your license has lapsed, do not write it as active, because a state board's lookup will show the real status to anyone who checks.

List a privilege under the Occupational Therapy Licensure Compact only if you actually hold one.

## Name the setting you work in

The work changes with the setting, so name yours near the top and give each one its own details.

**Inpatient rehab and acute care.** Show diagnoses, length of stay, team conferences, and discharge planning. In acute care, name the units you covered, such as the intensive care unit (ICU), neuro, or orthopedics.

**Skilled nursing.** Show your caseload, the population, and your work with Section GG, the functional items of the federal MDS (Minimum Data Set) assessment. Name any rehab software you used.

**Outpatient and hand therapy.** Show patients per day, the conditions you treated, and any orthoses you fabricated. Include post-surgical protocols if you followed them.

**Home health.** Show visits per day, your territory, home safety work, and equipment training. If you completed OASIS (Outcome and Assessment Information Set) assessments, say so.

**Schools and early intervention.** Show your caseload, grade levels, and your work on IEPs or IFSPs (individualized family service plans). School therapists work under IDEA (the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act), so name it, and say whether you worked directly with students, consulted with teachers, or both.

**Before:** "School-based occupational therapist supporting students with special needs."

**After:** "School-based occupational therapist, OTR/L ([state]); caseload of [42] students in grades [K-5] across [3] schools; wrote and updated IEP goals under IDEA; direct service and teacher consultation."

**Pediatric clinics.** Show age ranges, feeding or sensory work, and how you trained parents to carry the work home.

**Mental health.** Show the program type, group size, and the coping, routine, and life-skills work you ran.

Occupational therapy assistants carry out treatment under a therapist's supervision and have their own section in our [physical therapist assistant resume guide](/guides/physical-therapist-assistant-resume/). An occupational therapist's page should show the part the therapist is responsible for: evaluating, setting goals, writing the plan, and deciding when it should change.

## Credentials, exactly as held

Our guide on [how to list certifications on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume/) covers where they go. Beyond your OTR and license, list only what you hold.

- **CHT.** The Certified Hand Therapist credential comes from HTCC (the Hand Therapy Certification Commission). It is open to occupational and physical therapists, and you need at least 4,000 hours of hand therapy practice to sit for its exam.
- **AOTA certifications.** If you hold a board or specialty certification from AOTA (the American Occupational Therapy Association), write it exactly as it was issued, with its current status.
- **ATP.** The Assistive Technology Professional credential comes from RESNA (the Rehabilitation Engineering and Assistive Technology Society of North America). It fits seating and mobility work.
- **Training certificates.** Write the course name and issuer for training such as lymphedema, neuro-developmental treatment, or LSVT BIG (Lee Silverman Voice Treatment BIG). If your training comes with a renewal date, as LSVT BIG does, write the date it runs through. A course you attended is different from a credential you earned, so describe it accurately.
- **BLS.** Write the issuer and the date your Basic Life Support card runs through.

## Your evaluations, your treatment, and your charting

**Evaluations.** Say how many you complete each week, and whether you evaluated independently. New therapists should say so if a mentor co-signed.

**Assessment tools.** Name them. Common examples include the COPM (Canadian Occupational Performance Measure), the Allen Cognitive Level Screen, the MoCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment), AM-PAC (Activity Measure for Post-Acute Care) "6-Clicks," the Beery VMI (Beery-Buktenica Developmental Test of Visual-Motor Integration), and the Sensory Profile 2. List only the tools you have used with real patients.

**What you treated.** Name ADL retraining, cognition, vision, upper extremity function, feeding and swallowing where your training and state allow it, transfers, adaptive equipment, and caregiver training. "Worked on functional goals" gives a hiring manager nothing to picture.

**Documentation and payers.** Say which rules you documented under, such as Medicare Part A, Medicare Part B, or school-based billing. If your employer tracked productivity and you met the target, you can state that number.

**Leadership.** List fieldwork students you supervised, assistants you directed, programs you started, and any in-services you taught.

**Systems.** Name WebPT, Net Health, Epic, Oracle Health (formerly Cerner), PointClickCare, or whatever you charted in, because screening software looks for product names.

## New graduates: write your fieldwork like a job

Give each Level II placement the same care you would give a job. Name the setting, the population, how long you were there, and how much of the caseload you carried by the end.

**Before:** "Completed Level II fieldwork in a skilled nursing facility."

**After:** "Level II fieldwork, [120]-bed skilled nursing facility, [12] weeks; carried [6] patients per day by the end under [an OTR/L fieldwork educator]; completed [8] evaluations with co-signature; ADL retraining and discharge planning."

## Format notes

**Length follows your experience.** One page is right for a new graduate. Two pages is normal once you have more than one substantive role to describe. Put your license and certification near the top in their own labeled section, and start each job with your evaluation line.

Federal applications through USAJOBS generally cap at two pages as of the September 2025 OPM (Office of Personnel Management) change. The VA (Department of Veterans Affairs) says that limit does not apply to its occupational therapist jobs, so follow the resume instructions in the job announcement.

Put your newest job first. Use one column, standard headings, and real text, and leave out graphics and tables, so screening software can find your setting and your license.

## Occupational therapy bullets you can adapt, keeping only what's true

- "Occupational therapist, OTR/L ([state]); [setting]; [N] patients per day and [N] evaluations per week"
- "Evaluated [population] with [COPM / MoCA / AM-PAC] and wrote individualized plans of care with measurable goals"
- "Provided ADL retraining, [upper extremity / cognitive / vision] treatment, and caregiver training for [diagnoses]"
- "Fabricated [custom orthoses] and followed [surgeon] post-operative protocols for [N] patients per week"
- "Managed a school caseload of [N] students in grades [range]; wrote and updated IEP goals under IDEA; consulted with [N] teachers"
- "Completed home safety assessments and adaptive equipment training on [N] visits per day across [territory]"
- "Supervised [N] Level II fieldwork students and directed [N] occupational therapy assistants"
- "Documented in [system] under [Medicare Part A / Part B / school-based] rules"

Keep a bullet only if you could walk a rehab manager through it.

## What screening software looks for on an occupational therapist resume

Terms that often appear in occupational therapy postings include occupational therapist, OTR/L, NBCOT, state license, evaluation, plan of care, activities of daily living (ADLs), instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), upper extremity, cognition, adaptive equipment, orthotic fabrication, discharge planning, caregiver training, Section GG, IEP, and your setting and systems by name. Use a term only if you have done the work behind it.

[The U.S. Department of Labor's Occupational Information Network, or O*NET, lists this work under Occupational Therapists (29-1122.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/29-1122.00), and its task list can remind you of work you did and left off. On your resume, use the professional title your license gives you.

The riskiest lines overstate your status or your skills. A lapsed license written as active or a certification you never finished can be checked quickly, and a treatment area outside your training tends to come out when someone asks you about it.
