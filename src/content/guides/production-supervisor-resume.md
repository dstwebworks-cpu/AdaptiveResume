A production supervisor resume is read by a plant manager or an operations manager who thinks in shift numbers. How many people did you run, on how many lines, and what did the shift produce? What was the scrap rate, the changeover time, the overall equipment effectiveness? How many recordables? A resume that says "supervised production staff to meet daily goals" answers none of that and reads like every other supervisor resume in the folder.

The supervisors who get called first write the crew size, the output, and the standards their line runs under, and they put a number on the thing they improved. Those numbers are already in your shift reports.

## Lead with the crew and the shift numbers

**Before:** "Production supervisor responsible for overseeing daily operations and ensuring production targets were met."

**After:** "Second-shift production supervisor for a 22-person crew on three packaging lines: 180,000 units a shift, scrap reduced from 4.1% to 2.3% in one year, overall equipment effectiveness raised from 61% to 74%, zero recordable injuries in [N] months. Line runs under SQF and OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147 lockout/tagout."

The second version gives the reader the crew, the equipment, the output, two improved numbers, the safety record, and the standards in one block. Everything in it came from one supervisor's shift reports and the plant's quality system.

## The parts of the job that carry the most weight

**Crew and shift.** Head count, shift pattern, direct and indirect reports, and whether you handled scheduling, attendance, and performance reviews. "22 direct reports on second shift, scheduling and reviews included" is a complete answer.

**Output and efficiency.** Units per shift, throughput, overall equipment effectiveness (OEE), changeover times, and on-time completion against the schedule. If your plant ran a manufacturing execution system or tracked OEE in software, name it.

**Quality.** Scrap and rework rates, first-pass yield, customer complaints, and the quality system the line runs under: ISO 9001 for general manufacturing, IATF 16949 in automotive, SQF, BRCGS, or FSSC 22000 in food, current good manufacturing practice (cGMP) in pharmaceutical and medical device plants. Write the system your plant is actually certified to.

**Safety.** Recordable incident rate, days without a lost-time injury, lockout/tagout and machine guarding compliance, safety audits, and the training you delivered. OSHA 30-hour General Industry is the card plants look for at the supervisor level.

**Continuous improvement.** 5S, standard work, Kaizen events you led, root-cause analysis, and the result of each in a number. A supervisor who can write "led a changeover Kaizen that cut setup time from 45 to 28 minutes" has written a line that gets the interview.

**People development.** Operators trained and cross-trained, leads you developed, and turnover on your shift compared with the plant.

## Certifications and training, exactly as held

OSHA 30-hour (or 10-hour) General Industry card with the year. Lean and Six Sigma belts with the granting organization (an employer program, ASQ, or another body) and the year. Forklift or powered industrial truck certification if you operate. Food safety training such as HACCP or Preventive Controls Qualified Individual (PCQI) in food plants. APICS credentials from ASCM (the Certified in Planning and Inventory Management, CPIM, is a single exam) if you hold one. Training you completed without a credential is listed as training, with the provider.

## Industry notes

**Automotive and tier suppliers:** IATF 16949, production part approval process (PPAP), layered process audits, takt time, and line balancing. **Food and beverage:** SQF or BRCGS, sanitation and changeover, allergen control, washdown-rated equipment, and HACCP. **Packaging and consumer goods:** high-speed lines, changeovers, and OEE. **Pharmaceutical and medical device:** cGMP, batch records, deviations, and validated equipment. **Process industries:** batch versus continuous, control rooms, and process safety. Write your industry's standards by name; a plant manager reads them as proof you have lived under them.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** A supervisor with two plants, a promotion from lead, and a Lean project history has more that matters than one page holds. **Two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive job to describe.** Keep the crew, the numbers, the standards, and the improvements; cut the duties every supervisor shares.

Reverse chronological, single column, a promotion shown as two entries under one employer, and no graphics or photos. Corporate plants run applications through screening software before a manager sees them, and a two-column template can scramble the read. One exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Supervised a [N]-person crew on [N] [lines/cells] across [shift]; scheduling, attendance, and reviews"
- "Produced [N] units a shift against a target of [N]; on-time schedule completion of [N]%"
- "Reduced scrap from [N]% to [N]% and raised OEE from [N]% to [N]% in [N] months"
- "Led a [changeover/5S/Kaizen] event that cut [setup time/downtime] from [N] to [N] [minutes/hours]"
- "Zero recordable injuries in [N] months; delivered [lockout/tagout, machine guarding] training to [N] operators"
- "Ran the line under [ISO 9001/IATF 16949/SQF/cGMP]; passed [N] audits with no major findings"
- "Cross-trained [N] operators on [N] machines; shift turnover of [N]% against a plant average of [N]%"
- "OSHA 30-hour General Industry, [year]; Lean Six Sigma Green Belt, [organization], [year]"

## What screening software looks for on a production supervisor resume

Filters commonly read for: production supervisor, shift supervisor, manufacturing, direct reports, OEE, throughput, scrap, first-pass yield, changeover, 5S, Lean, Six Sigma, Kaizen, continuous improvement, root cause, standard work, ISO 9001, IATF 16949, SQF, GMP, HACCP, lockout/tagout, OSHA 30, safety, scheduling, and the ERP or MES names in the posting (SAP, Oracle, or a plant system). [O*NET's profile for first-line supervisors of production and operating workers](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/51-1011.00) has the full task list.

The highest-risk lines are the ones a plant verifies through your references: a head count you did not lead, a number your last plant will not confirm, a belt you did not earn, or a safety record the plant's log does not show. Write what the shift reports show.
