A construction worker resume is read by a foreman, a superintendent, or a recruiter for a general contractor or a staffing company. They are looking for four things: whether you can work safely on their site, which tools and equipment you can handle, what kind of jobs you have worked, and whether you show up every day. A resume that says "hard-working laborer with construction experience" tells them none of that.

Construction work leaves a record: the cards in your wallet, the projects you were on, the equipment you were trained on, and the days you worked. Put that record on the page.

## Lead with your cards, your work, and your reliability

**Before:** "Construction laborer responsible for site cleanup, carrying materials, and assisting tradesmen."

**After:** "Construction laborer for a [commercial general contractor] on [3] projects, including a [4]-story steel-frame office building: concrete pours, forming and stripping, and site cleanup on a [12]-person crew; OSHA 10 ([year]), trained on the [skid steer] and [scissor lift]; [18] months with no missed days and no recordable injuries."

## Safety training, named exactly

**OSHA outreach cards.** The OSHA 10-hour and 30-hour construction courses are part of OSHA's [Outreach Training Program](https://www.osha.gov/training/outreach), which federal OSHA treats as voluntary. Some places make them mandatory. Nevada, for example, requires construction workers to get a 10-hour card and supervisors a 30-hour card [within 15 days of being hired](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-618.html#NRS618Sec983). New York City requires [Site Safety Training (SST) cards](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/safety/sst-worker-information.page) with at least 40 hours of training for workers on many larger sites. Write the card, the year, and for an SST card, the expiration date.

**Fall protection, scaffolds, and trenches.** The construction rules require fall protection where a worker on an unprotected edge could fall [6 feet or more](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/1926.501) to a lower level (scaffolds have their own 10-foot rule), and a protective system, such as sloping, shoring, or a trench box, in any trench or excavation [5 feet deep or more](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/1926.652) unless it is cut entirely in stable rock. If you were trained in fall protection, scaffold use, or trench safety, name the training. A contractor reads it as one less thing to teach.

**Other cards worth listing.** Flagger certification, first aid and CPR, forklift or aerial lift training, silica awareness, confined space entry, and hazard communication training all belong on the resume with the issuer and the year. List only what you completed.

## Tools, equipment, and trades work

**Equipment.** Name what you can run: skid steers, mini excavators, compactors and rollers, concrete saws, jackhammers, power trowels, forklifts, scissor lifts, and boom lifts. Say whether you were trained and evaluated on it, because a foreman wants to know what they can hand you on day one.

**Tools and tasks.** Describe the work in the words the trades use: forming and stripping, rebar placement, concrete pours and finishing, demolition, framing support, drywall hanging, grading, trenching, pipe laying, and traffic control. If you helped a carpenter, an electrician, or a plumber, say which trade and what you did.

**Types of jobs.** Commercial, residential, road and bridge, utility, and industrial work are read differently. Name the type and the size when you know it, such as square footage, the number of stories, or the length of the road or pipe.

## The numbers a foreman reads

**Projects and crew.** Give the number of projects, their type and size, and the size of your crew.

**Attendance.** Months without a missed day, early starts, and overtime worked show reliability, which a foreman weighs heavily.

**Safety record.** Write your months or years without a recordable injury and any safety recognition. If you served on a safety committee or did the morning stretch-and-flex or toolbox talks, say so.

**Production.** If your crew tracked it, write what you placed, poured, or installed in a day, such as cubic yards of concrete or feet of pipe.

## Starting out with no construction experience

Many people come into construction from warehouse, landscaping, moving, factory, or military work. Those jobs show what a foreman needs: physical work on a schedule, safe use of equipment, and showing up on time. Write them with their numbers and the equipment you ran, then add the safety training you have completed. An OSHA 10 card, first aid and CPR, and a forklift evaluation are each a real line that tells a contractor you are ready for a job site. A pre-apprenticeship program or a union laborers' training course belongs here too, with its name and dates.

## Moving up

Lines that point upward matter even for an entry-level job: being made lead on a small crew, running a piece of equipment the crew depends on, training new laborers, or entering an apprenticeship. ## Format notes

**Length follows your history.** A construction worker with one or two employers usually fits on one page. **Two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive job to describe,** such as several contractors, a staffing history, or military construction work. Keep the cards, the equipment, the projects, and the numbers; cut the duties every site shares.

Use reverse chronological order and a single column, and leave out graphics and photos. Many large contractors and staffing companies run applications through hiring software, and a two-column template can scramble the read. Put your safety cards in their own section near the top, where a foreman sees them first.

Seasonal work and short gaps between projects are normal in construction. Write each job with its dates and give a gap one plain dated line.

## Construction worker resume examples: a bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Laborer on [N] [commercial/residential] projects for [contractor]; forming, stripping, and concrete pours on a [N]-person crew"
- "OSHA 10 construction ([year]); fall protection and scaffold training through [employer], [year]"
- "Ran a [skid steer/mini excavator] for grading and trenching; trained and evaluated by [employer], [year]"
- "Placed and finished about [N] cubic yards of concrete a week on slab and sidewalk work"
- "Flagger certified through [issuer], [year]; ran traffic control on [N] road projects"
- "Helped the [electrical/plumbing] crew pull wire or lay pipe on [N] projects"
- "[N] months with no missed days and no recordable injuries"
- "Made crew lead on [N] jobs; trained [N] new laborers"

## What screening software looks for on a construction worker resume

Filters commonly read for: construction worker, construction laborer, general laborer, site work, concrete, forming, demolition, framing, drywall, grading, trenching, excavation, pipe laying, flagger, traffic control, OSHA 10, OSHA 30, SST, fall protection, scaffold, skid steer, excavator, forklift, aerial lift, power tools, blueprints, safety, and teamwork. The highest-risk lines are the ones a contractor can check with one call or by asking to see your wallet: a card you never earned, equipment you were never trained on, or a project you were not on. Write what you actually did.
