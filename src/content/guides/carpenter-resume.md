Carpentry hiring runs on one question: what can you actually build, and how much of it have you done? A superintendent reading your resume wants to know whether you can lay out a wall from the plans, frame it plumb and square, and hand off a room that passes punch list. Generic language ("skilled carpenter with a strong work ethic and attention to detail") doesn't answer that. The specifics do — the kind of carpentry, the size of what you built, the crew you worked with, and the training you hold exactly as issued.

The other thing that costs carpenters on paper is scope blur. Rough framing, finish and trim, concrete formwork, cabinet installation, and commercial interiors are related but different jobs, and a resume that says "carpenter, all phases" competes badly against one that names the phases and puts numbers next to them.

## The scope line: your work in one sentence

Under each job, give one line another carpenter would recognize instantly.

**Before:** "Carpenter responsible for framing, finish work, and other duties on residential and commercial projects."

**After:** "Lead carpenter on a [4]-person crew; rough framing and layout on [12] single-family homes per year ([2,400]–[3,200] sq ft each), plus interior trim, door hanging, and punch-list closeout; read architectural plans and set layout from them."

## Credentials and training, exactly as held

Carpentry has few required credentials, so the ones you hold get read closely. Our guide on [how to list certifications on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume/) covers placement and format; here is what typically belongs in a carpenter's block, listed with the issuing body and year.

- **Apprenticeship and journeyman status.** A UBC (United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners of America) apprenticeship is a registered apprenticeship with the U.S. Department of Labor; most run four years, with curriculum from the CITF (Carpenters International Training Fund) through regional training centers. Completing it makes you a journeyman carpenter — write "Journeyman Carpenter — [training center], [year]," or "Carpenter Apprentice, [year] of [N]" if you're still in it. Non-union registered apprenticeships count the same way; name the sponsor.
- **NCCER Carpentry.** The NCCER (National Center for Construction Education and Research) structures its carpentry curriculum in three tiers — General Carpentry; Advanced Carpentry: Frame & Finish; and Form Carpentry — with NCCER Core as the prerequisite. List the tier completed and the year.
- **OSHA 10-hour or 30-hour construction card.** OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) runs the Outreach Training Program; the 10-hour class is for workers, the 30-hour for supervisors or anyone with safety responsibility. Write "OSHA 30-Hour Construction, [year]." OSHA itself says the card is not a certification or license, so don't call it one.
- **Scaffold training.** OSHA's construction scaffold standard, 29 CFR 1926 Subpart L, requires anyone who works on a scaffold to be trained by a qualified person, and anyone who erects, moves, or dismantles one to be trained by a competent person. If your employer has designated you the scaffold competent person, say so — it's worth a line.
- **Fall protection training.** OSHA's construction fall protection standard, 29 CFR 1926 Subpart M, requires training for workers exposed to fall hazards. Name the training and year.
- **Aerial lift / MEWP training.** Boom lifts fall under OSHA's construction aerial lift rule (29 CFR 1926.453); OSHA treats scissor lifts as mobile scaffolds under the scaffold standard (29 CFR 1926.452(w)). The ANSI/SAIA A92.24 training standard for mobile elevating work platforms covers both. List equipment type and year.
- **First aid and CPR.** Typically through the American Red Cross or the American Heart Association; list the current-through date.
- **ICRA training.** ICRA (Infection Control Risk Assessment) training covers construction inside occupied hospitals and clinics; the UBC runs its own program, UBC ICRA Best Practices in Health Care Construction — a 24-hour course that issues a card. If you've built containment barriers under negative-air protocols in a live healthcare facility, the credential plus that experience line is a real differentiator.
- **State contractor license.** Only if you hold one — that's for carpenters who run their own work, and the rules vary by state. Name the state and the license class as your state names it.

Nothing you're "about to take" unless it's labeled that way — "OSHA 30, scheduled [month/year]" is honest. A misnamed credential loses ground fast with a reader who holds the same one.

## Carpenter resume skills: the parts of the job that carry weight

Most carpenter resumes get this section wrong by listing nouns ("framing, trim, power tools") instead of work. Write each skill as something you did, at a size, with a tool or a plan behind it.

**Rough framing and layout.** Wall, floor, and roof framing; stairs; setting layout from the plans with a tape, chalk line, and level. Say the type of construction — wood-frame residential, light-gauge metal stud, engineered lumber — and the scale: homes per year, square feet, stories.

**Concrete formwork.** Wall forms, footings, slabs, columns, stripping and resetting gang forms. Name the form system ([system]) and the pour sizes — formwork is its own specialty and often filtered for by name.

**Finish and trim.** Baseboard, casing, crown, wainscot, stair rail and balusters, window and door trim. Finish carpentry is judged on fit, so say the level — production tract trim versus custom work — and the materials: paint-grade, stain-grade, hardwood.

**Doors, hardware, and cabinetry.** Pre-hung and slab doors, commercial hollow-metal frames, hardware sets and closers, kitchen and bath cabinets, countertops, built-ins. Count them: "[N] cabinet installs."

**Drywall and related work.** Drywall, acoustical ceilings, insulation, siding, decks, windows — list what you've actually done, but not ahead of your core carpentry.

**Plans and code.** Reading architectural and structural drawings, taking off materials from them, and working under the IRC (International Residential Code) or IBC (International Building Code), both published by the International Code Council. Name only the code your work was actually inspected against.

**Tools.** Name the ones you run: framing nailers, miter and table saws, track saws, routers, laser levels, powder-actuated fasteners if you hold the tool-specific training. Skip "power tools."

**Leading a crew and closing out.** Crew size, apprentices trained, material ordering, coordinating with other trades, punch-list closeout. If you were the lead, say how many people you led. ## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** Two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive carpentry job to describe; one page is right for apprentices and people entering the trade. Order matters more than length: credentials block near the top in its own labeled section (screening software keys on it), then experience with a scope line atop each job. If you're trimming, cut older non-trade jobs before a single carpentry specific.

One genuine exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change. That's a hard requirement, not a style preference.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, standard headings, real text rather than graphics or tables — hiring systems parse plain layouts most reliably. Skip the graphics and the photo.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Lead carpenter on a [N]-person crew; rough framing on [N] [homes/units] per year, [N] sq ft each"
- "Set layout from architectural plans; framed walls, floors, and roofs to IRC on [wood-frame/engineered] construction"
- "Formed and stripped [footings/walls/columns] for pours up to [N] cubic yards using [system] forms"
- "Installed [paint-grade/stain-grade] trim, casing, crown, and stair rail on [N] custom homes"
- "Hung [N] commercial hollow-metal doors and hardware sets on a [N]-story [project type]"
- "Built ICRA containment barriers and worked under negative-air protocols on a [N]-bed hospital renovation"
- "Trained [N] apprentices; ran punch-list closeout on [N] units to the superintendent's sign-off"

Each bullet is a frame, not a claim. If a line isn't true of your work, it doesn't go on your page — a superintendent's first question on site will find it.

## What screening software looks for on a carpenter resume

Hiring systems read for the vocabulary of the trade, and a human reads for the same words minutes later. The terms that typically matter: carpenter, journeyman, apprentice, rough framing, layout, formwork, concrete forms, finish carpentry, trim, cabinet installation, doors and hardware, drywall, metal stud, blueprint reading, IRC, IBC, OSHA 10, OSHA 30, scaffold, fall protection, aerial lift, punch list, and lead carpenter.
