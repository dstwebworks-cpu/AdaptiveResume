An accountant resume has four questions to answer near the top. Which kind of accounting did you do? Are you a licensed CPA (certified public accountant), and is that license active? Which accounting rules did you apply yourself? And what part of the financial close did you own? A resume that says "responsible for month-end close and financial reporting" answers none of those questions.

The title alone tells a hiring manager very little. Three people can all be called an accountant and spend their days on entirely different work, one on tax returns, one on audits, and one on a company's revenue. This guide shows you how to put your real work on the page, in words a hiring manager and screening software can both find.

## Your accounting in one sentence

Put one line under each job that tells a controller what you owned.

**Before:** "Accountant responsible for month-end close, reconciliations, and financial reporting."

**After:** "Senior accountant, CPA ([state], active); owned [revenue and fixed assets] in a [6]-day close for a [manufacturer with more than $100M in revenue] on [NetSuite]; applied [Accounting Standards Codification (ASC) 606 revenue recognition rules]; performed [14] key controls under the Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) with [no] audit exceptions in [2] years."

Write your numbers from memory and from records you own. Do not go back into a former employer's accounting system to collect figures. Do not name an audit or tax client, and do not print a private company's revenue; a range and an industry tell a hiring manager enough. If you signed a confidentiality or severance agreement, read it before you write any figure, because it is a contract and breaking it can put your severance at risk. The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission [explains what it means to give up legal claims in a severance agreement](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/qa-understanding-waivers-discrimination-claims-employee-severance-agreements), and an employment attorney can tell you what yours allows.

## Say which kind of accounting you do

Accounting work differs a lot from one kind to the next, so say which kind is yours near the top of the page.

**Public accounting** means audit, tax, or advisory work for clients of a CPA firm. Show your level, your busy seasons, and the industries you served.

**Corporate accounting** means keeping one company's books and reports. Show the accounts you owned, the entities you consolidated, and the close calendar you worked to.

**Tax accounting** means preparing returns or planning for a firm or a corporate tax department. Show the return types, the volume, and the states or countries involved.

**Government and nonprofit accounting** runs on its own rules. Show the funds you handled, the grants you tracked, and any single audit you supported.

Here is the same kind of before-and-after for a tax job and an audit job.

**Before:** "Tax accountant who prepared returns for individuals and businesses."

**After:** "Tax senior; prepared [240] [1040, 1065, and 1120] returns per season for [owner-operated businesses] across [6] states on [CCH Axcess]; returns reviewed by [a tax manager]."

**Before:** "Staff auditor who performed audit procedures for clients."

**After:** "Audit senior; ran the [revenue and inventory] sections on [8] [manufacturing] audits per year under [American Institute of CPAs (AICPA)] standards; supervised [2] staff auditors."

Two neighboring jobs get their own guides. If you keep the day-to-day books and hand them to an outside CPA, our [bookkeeper resume guide](/guides/bookkeeper-resume/) fits you better. If your work is budgets and forecasts about the future, read our [financial analyst resume guide](/guides/financial-analyst-resume/). An accountant's page is about recording, testing, and reporting what actually happened, under a named set of rules.

## Credentials, exactly as held

Our guide on [how to list certifications on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume/) covers where they go. Here is what belongs in an accountant's credentials section.

- **CPA.** A state board of accountancy issues this license after you pass the Uniform CPA Examination and meet that state's other requirements. Write the state and the status. Many states have added a route of a bachelor's degree plus two years of experience beside the traditional 150-hour route, so check your own board's current rule. NASBA (the National Association of State Boards of Accountancy) runs a free public lookup called CPAverify, and employers can use it to check a license.
- **CMA.** The Certified Management Accountant credential comes from IMA (the Institute of Management Accountants). It requires a bachelor's degree or related professional certification, a two-part exam, and two continuous years of professional experience, and it fits cost and management accounting.
- **CIA.** The Certified Internal Auditor credential comes from The IIA (The Institute of Internal Auditors). It requires internal audit experience and a three-part exam, and certain credential holders can take a single challenge exam instead. It fits internal audit work.
- **EA.** An enrolled agent passes the IRS (Internal Revenue Service) three-part Special Enrollment Examination, or qualifies through past IRS experience. Enrolled agents have unlimited rights to represent taxpayers before the IRS, and that is worth saying if you did representation work.

Write only what you hold today, and write its real status. If you are partway through an exam, say how many sections you have passed.

## Your close, your standards, and your systems

**The close.** Say how many days your close took, which accounts you owned, and whether you prepared or reviewed the entries. Include reconciliations, accruals, and intercompany eliminations when they were yours.

**The rules you applied.** Name the standard instead of writing "ensured compliance." U.S. GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) is organized in the FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board) Accounting Standards Codification, or ASC. Common topics include ASC 606 for revenue, ASC 842 for leases, ASC 718 for stock compensation, and ASC 740 for income taxes. State and local government accountants work under GASB (Governmental Accounting Standards Board) rules, federal accountants work under FASAB (Federal Accounting Standards Advisory Board) standards, and companies abroad may report under IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards). List only the topics you actually worked on.

**Audits and internal controls.** External auditors should name their standards: AICPA (American Institute of CPAs) standards for private companies and PCAOB (Public Company Accounting Oversight Board) standards for public ones. Government auditors can name Government Auditing Standards, often called the Yellow Book. Corporate accountants can show the controls they performed under SOX Section 404 and the outside audits they supported.

**Reporting.** Say what you prepared and who read it. SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) filings such as the 10-K and 10-Q are different work from a monthly management package, so say which one you did.

**Tax.** Name the forms, such as the 1040, 1065, 1120, and 990, along with your annual volume and whether you prepared or reviewed them.

**Systems.** Name the real products. Corporate accountants may use NetSuite, SAP, Oracle, Workday, Sage Intacct, BlackLine, or FloQast. Tax accountants may use CCH Axcess, UltraTax CS, or Lacerte. Screening software looks for product names, and "strong accounting software skills" gives it nothing to find.

## A staff accountant resume: what to show early on

A staff accountant usually has less scope to write about, so the details matter more. Name the journal entries you prepared, the reconciliations you owned, and the audit requests you answered. Say which entries a senior accountant reviewed, because preparing an entry and reviewing it are different work.

If you are new, your internship and your coursework can carry real weight. Write what you did on the internship, list the systems you touched, and show your CPA exam progress plainly. A class project does not count as running a real close, so do not write it as one.

## Format notes

**Your history sets the length.** One page is right for a new graduate or for a few years at one employer. Two pages is normal once you have more than one substantive role to describe. Put your license and credentials near the top in their own labeled section, and start each job with your one-line summary of what you owned.

Most federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages as of the September 2025 OPM (Office of Personnel Management) change, so plan on two pages if you apply for a federal accounting job.

Put your current job first and list the earlier ones after it. Use one column, standard headings, and real text, and leave out graphics and tables, so screening software can find your standards and your systems.

## Accounting bullets you can adapt, keeping only what's true

- "Owned [revenue / fixed assets / accruals] in a [N]-day close; prepared [N] monthly journal entries in [system]"
- "Reconciled [N] balance sheet accounts monthly and cleared [aged items] within [N] days"
- "Applied [ASC 606 / ASC 842] to [contract type]; documented conclusions for [N] [contracts / leases]"
- "Performed [N] SOX key controls; supported [external auditor type] testing with [no] deficiencies in [year]"
- "Consolidated [N] entities in [system]; prepared intercompany eliminations and [currency] translation"
- "Prepared [N] [1065 / 1120 / 1040] returns per season across [N] states; [reviewed by / reviewed for] [level]"
- "Audited [N] [industry] clients under [AICPA / PCAOB] standards; led [section] on engagements of [N] people"
- "Prepared [10-K / 10-Q / management package] sections covering [area] for [audience]"

Keep a bullet only if you could walk a controller through it.

## What screening software looks for on an accountant resume

Terms that often appear in accounting postings include accountant, CPA, GAAP, month-end close, general ledger, journal entries, account reconciliation, accruals, consolidations, financial statements, variance analysis, SOX, internal controls, external audit, ASC 606, ASC 842, tax returns, Excel, and your systems by name. Use a term only if you have done the work behind it.

[The U.S. Department of Labor's Occupational Information Network, or O*NET, lists this work under Accountants and Auditors (13-2011.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/13-2011.00), one code that covers both titles, so say near the top of your page which of the two you are.

The riskiest lines overstate your license or your authority. "CPA" without a license, "reviewed" when you only prepared, or "led the audit" when you ran one section can all be checked in a single phone call or a license lookup.
