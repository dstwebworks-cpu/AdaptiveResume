Outplacement assistance has an odd shape. The employer buys it, the employee receives it, and the two almost never compare notes on what was delivered. HR sees a proposal and a usage report. The employee sees an email with a login and, a week or two later, a resume. Neither side sees the whole thing, which is why this guide is written for both.

## What a package usually contains

A standard outplacement package has five parts, and they are not equal.

1. **A resume.** Either rewritten by a writer, or a template plus a coach who reviews what the employee writes.
2. **Coaching.** A fixed number of sessions, by phone or video, on strategy and interviews.
3. **A job-search platform.** A login with listings, networking tools, and sometimes practice interviews.
4. **Check-ins.** Someone who follows up and records whether the employee engaged.
5. **A report to the employer.** Usage figures, and sometimes claims about outcomes.

One large provider's published rate card lists packages with a custom-written resume and coaching at about $1,900 to $2,600 per person. Another large provider's published card starts at $2,400 per person and rises to $11,310 for executives. Most providers quote privately, so the employer often does not know what a comparable package costs elsewhere.

## What employees actually use

Employees use the resume first and most. It is the thing the employee sends out that week, and the only part of the package with a deadline attached. Coaching gets used by people who have not searched for a job in a long time and skipped by people who searched last year. The platform login is opened once by many people and never again. The check-ins are for the report.

The most-cited public figure, a 2009 survey, found that only about 40 percent of employees offered outplacement used it, and that figure is dated enough that we treat it as a caution rather than a fact. Whatever the true number is, a program is only as good as the resume that comes out of it, because that is the part almost everyone touches.

## The resume piece, done right and done badly

Here is where the employer and the employee should compare notes, because this is where the money goes and where the value is decided.

**Done badly:** the employee uploads the old resume, and it comes back in the provider's template with the same sentences. The employee has a nicer-looking version of the same thin document. Nothing was found, because a template cannot find anything.

**Done right:** someone works backwards from what the employee actually did. The systems they ran, the standards they worked under, the people they trained, the numbers they owned. Each of those becomes a suggested line, the employee confirms or edits it, and the finished resume says what the person actually did at its real size.

This is a sample worker, not a real person.

**Before:** "Senior accountant. Responsible for month-end close and financial reporting."

**After:** "Senior accountant for a 3-entity manufacturing group: ran month-end close in NetSuite to a 5-day calendar, prepared consolidated financials reviewed by outside auditors with no adjustments in 3 years, managed fixed assets of about $18 million, and trained 2 staff accountants on the close checklist."

In a real rebuild, nothing in the second version would be invented: the entities, the system, the close calendar, the audit record, and the asset base would all be true and all missing. A critique would have said "add detail." A template would have kept the first line.

## What the employer should ask for

If you are buying outplacement assistance, ask for the resume piece by name and hold it to four requirements:

- **It rebuilds, it does not reformat.** Ask the provider how it finds what the employee left off.
- **The employee confirms every line.** Ask how that is recorded.
- **Licenses and certifications are asked about, never assumed.** Ask what happens to a credential the employee never mentioned.
- **The finished file reads cleanly to screening software.** Single column, standard headings, no graphics.

If the provider cannot answer the first and third plainly, the resume piece is formatting, and you should price it as formatting.

## What the employee should do in week one

If you have just been offered outplacement, do three things in the first week. Use the resume piece immediately, because it is the part you will send out. Read the finished resume as a stranger would, and ask whether it says what you actually did at its real size; if it does not, say so and ask for more. And decide about coaching honestly: if you searched for a job in the last two years, you may not need it; if it has been a decade, take every session.

## What the first three weeks should look like

Outplacement assistance is only useful if it moves at the speed of a job search, so it helps both sides to know what a working timeline looks like.

**Week one.** The employee receives a plain message that says what will happen next, and the resume work starts. If the program rebuilds resumes, the employee is answering questions about their real work this week, and the employer should ask the provider when the finished, confirmed resume will be in the employee's hands. If the program critiques or reformats, the employee is uploading the old resume and waiting.

**Week two.** The finished resume goes out. The employee applies to the first postings, and the first coaching session, if the program includes one, happens now, while there is something concrete to talk about. A first session that lands after the resume exists has something concrete to work from.

**Week three.** The employee is adjusting: the resume is tailored to a specific posting or two, and the coach, if there is one, is working on interviews. This is also the week the employer should ask the provider for its first report and check that it counts finished resumes, not logins.

A program that has not produced a resume by the end of week two is one the employee is likely to stop using, and the coaching hours in the package do not change that.

## What a severance agreement should say about it

If outplacement assistance is written into a severance agreement, the wording decides what the employee can expect and what the employer has to deliver. "Career transition assistance" is broad enough to be satisfied by almost anything. A specific clause names the provider or the type of service, the duration, and the components, and it is fairer to both sides. Have your attorney confirm the wording before you rely on it, and make sure the resume piece is described specifically enough that a template would not satisfy it.

## Format notes

A 20-year employee should not be forced onto one page; two pages is the normal result once there is more than one substantive role to describe. The file itself should be reverse chronological and one column, with no photos or graphics, so the first employer's software reads it in order. Anyone in the group applying to federal jobs is capped at two pages on USAJOBS under the OPM change effective 09/27/2025. On the notice side of a layoff, the U.S. Department of Labor's [plant closings and layoffs page](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/termination/plantclosings) states which employers are covered; notice and assistance are separate obligations.
