You found a posting that genuinely fits, and now you're staring at your resume knowing it was written for nobody in particular. Tailoring is how you fix that — and it has a bad reputation it doesn't deserve, because most advice makes it sound like keyword-stuffing. Done honestly, tailoring your resume to a job description changes almost no facts at all. It changes which facts lead, and what words carry them.

## Read the job description like a grader

Before touching your resume, sort the JD into two piles:

- **Must-haves** — the requirements the employer will actually screen on. They hide in plain sight: anything repeated more than once, anything in the first three bullets, anything with a number attached ("3+ years"), any named credential, license, or system, and everything under "Requirements" rather than "Nice to have."
- **Nice-to-haves** — everything else: the wish-list items, the "familiarity with" lines, the culture adjectives.

Most postings have three to six real must-haves. Write them down as a literal list — that list is your grading rubric, because it's the employer's.

## Mirror the JD's vocabulary — where it's true

Employers and screening software search for *their* words, and the same work goes by different names in different shops. If you write "client onboarding" and the JD says "customer implementation," a keyword search misses you even though you're a match.

So, for each must-have you genuinely meet: use the JD's exact term for it, and keep your original term too if it's the industry-standard one. This is translation, not invention. The line you may never cross: adding a term for something you haven't done. The keyword gets you found; the human reading two minutes later checks whether it's true, and an interview built on a pasted keyword collapses in the first five minutes.

## Reorder so the relevant material leads

This is the most underused tailoring move, and it's pure honesty — nothing changes but sequence:

- **Within each job**, move the bullets that hit this JD's must-haves to the top. Recruiters skim the first bullet or two of each role; make those the ones that answer this posting.
- **In your skills section**, put the JD's must-have skills first, not alphabetical order.
- **In your summary**, name the two or three strongest matches between your history and their requirements — specifically, not "results-driven professional."

## A worked mini-example

![Before and after example of an improved resume line - every line confirmed by the person](/img/guides/examples/tailor-resume-to-job-description-example.png)

*Illustrative example. On your resume, every line comes from your real history - proposed as a question, added only when you confirm it.*

The JD's must-haves: *customer implementation, Salesforce, renewal retention.* The candidate has done all three — but her resume says it in her old company's dialect.

**Before:** "Managed client accounts and helped new customers get set up in our CRM."

**After:** "Led customer implementations for ~30 new accounts/year in Salesforce; carried renewal responsibility for the book afterward."

Every fact in the rewrite was already hers: the setups were implementations, the CRM was Salesforce, she owned renewals. The tailoring supplied zero new claims — it swapped her shop's vocabulary for the JD's and put the load-bearing terms where a skim finds them. That's the entire method.

## The honest limits of tailoring a resume to a job description

Tailoring rearranges and renames what's true. It cannot manufacture a match that isn't there — and it shouldn't try:

- **A missing must-have stays missing.** Show your closest real experience and let the gap be visible. Some gaps are survivable; a discovered stretch never is.
- **Vocabulary mirroring has a floor.** If you have to squint to call what you did by their term, don't. "I configured user accounts" is not "systems administration."
- **Don't tailor your titles.** Your job title is a checkable fact — reference calls and background checks compare it. If your real title undersold the work, keep the title and let the bullets carry the truth.

If honest tailoring can't produce a decent match against the must-have list, that's information too: your fifteen minutes are better spent on the next posting.
