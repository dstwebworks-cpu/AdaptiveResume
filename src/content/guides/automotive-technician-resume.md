An automotive technician resume is read by a service manager who thinks in ASE areas and flat-rate hours. The first question is which systems you are certified in and which you actually work on. The second is whether you turn hours: how many flat-rate hours a week, at what efficiency, with how many comebacks. A resume that says "experienced in all aspects of automotive repair" answers neither, and it reads like every other resume in the stack.

The technicians who get called first write the certifications exactly, put numbers on their production, and name the diagnostic work that separates a technician from a parts changer. All of that is already true of your work. It just is not on the page yet.

## Lead with the ASE areas and the numbers

**Before:** "ASE certified technician with 8 years of experience in brakes, engines, and electrical."

**After:** "ASE Master Automobile Technician (A1–A8), current through [month/year]; L1 Advanced Engine Performance Specialist. Flat-rate technician at a [brand] dealership: about 55 flat-rate hours a week at 125% efficiency, comeback rate under 2%, drivability and electrical diagnosis on about a third of tickets."

The second version tells a service manager which areas you hold, that you are a producer, and that you diagnose rather than swap parts. Every number in it is one you can read off your pay stubs and your shop's management system.

## Certifications, by their exact names

**ASE areas.** List each automobile and light truck area you hold by letter and name: A1 Engine Repair, A2 Automatic Transmission/Transaxle, A3 Manual Drive Train and Axles, A4 Suspension and Steering, A5 Brakes, A6 Electrical/Electronic Systems, A7 Heating and Air Conditioning, A8 Engine Performance, A9 Light Vehicle Diesel Engines. Master Automobile Technician status means A1 through A8 are all current. Advanced level: L1 Advanced Engine Performance Specialist, L3 Light Duty Hybrid/Electric Vehicle Specialist. ASE certifications must be recertified every five years, so write the expiration.

**EV and hybrid safety.** If you have completed ASE's xEV Electrical Safety certification (Level 1 awareness or Level 2 technician), list it by name with the year. Dealerships with electric models screen for it, and so do fleets electrifying their vehicles.

**Refrigerant.** EPA Section 609 technician certification for motor vehicle air conditioning, required to service vehicle A/C for compensation. Write "Section 609," not "EPA certified," so it is not confused with the stationary Section 608 credential.

**State licenses and inspections.** Some states license technicians or inspection stations separately, and emissions and safety inspector licenses are state-issued. If you hold one, list the state, the license type, and the expiration.

**Manufacturer training.** Factory programs and certifications (for example, Toyota T-TEN, GM ASEP, Ford ASSET, and brand-specific master-technician tracks) are strong lines at a dealership of that brand and good lines anywhere. List the brand, the program, and the level reached.

## The parts of the job that carry the most weight

**Production numbers.** Flat-rate hours per week or per pay period, efficiency percentage, and comeback rate. If your shop pays hourly, use tickets per day and the mix of work. Service managers hire on these numbers because the shop's revenue is made of them.

**Diagnostic work.** Drivability, no-start, and electrical diagnosis; network and module communication faults; ADAS (advanced driver assistance system) calibration after windshield, alignment, or collision work; scan tools and lab scopes by name (factory tools, Snap-on, Autel, Pico). Name the make-specific diagnostic software you have used, because a dealership posting will ask for it.

**Systems and repairs.** Engines, transmissions, driveline, suspension and steering, brakes including ABS and electronic parking brakes, HVAC, electrical, and emissions. Write the ones you actually do, and mark the ones you lead on.

**Shop systems and process.** Repair-order and inspection software (for example, dealership management systems and digital multipoint inspection tools), parts lookup, and warranty documentation. Warranty-claim accuracy is a real skill at a dealership, and a technician who writes clean stories on warranty claims saves the shop money.

**Mentoring.** Apprentices and lube technicians you have trained, and any shop foreman or team lead duties.

## Dealership, independent, fleet, and quick service

Write the setting, because the work differs. A dealership resume leads with the brand, the factory training, and warranty work. An independent shop resume leads with the range of makes and the diagnostic mix. A fleet resume leads with the vehicle count, preventive maintenance schedules, and uptime. A quick-service resume leads with volume, inspection accuracy, and the upsell you found honestly. None of these is lesser; each has its own numbers.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** A technician with three shops, two brands, and a master certification has more that matters than one page holds. **Two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive job to describe.** Keep the certifications, the production numbers, the diagnostic work, and the systems; cut the duties every technician shares.

Reverse chronological, single column, the certification block near the top, and no graphics or photos. Dealership groups run applications through screening software, and a two-column template can scramble the read. One exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change, and federal mechanic postings ask for details a shop application does not.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "ASE certified in [A4, A5, A6, A8], current through [month/year]; registered to test [A1, A2]"
- "Turned about [N] flat-rate hours a week at [N]% efficiency with a comeback rate under [N]%"
- "Diagnosed drivability, no-start, and electrical faults with [scan tool/lab scope]; about [N]% of tickets were diagnosis"
- "Performed ADAS calibrations after [windshield/alignment] work on [N] vehicles a month"
- "EPA Section 609 certified; serviced [N] A/C systems a month including [refrigerant type]"
- "Completed [brand] factory training to [level]; performed warranty repairs with [N]% claim approval"
- "Maintained a fleet of [N] vehicles on a preventive maintenance schedule; [N]% uptime"
- "Trained [N] apprentices and lube technicians on inspections and shop safety"

## What screening software looks for on an automotive technician resume

Filters commonly read for: ASE certified, Master Technician, A1 through A9, L1, L3, EPA 609, diagnostics, drivability, electrical, scan tool, lab scope, ADAS calibration, hybrid, EV, high voltage, brakes, suspension, alignment, transmission, HVAC, emissions, state inspection, flat rate, multipoint inspection, warranty, and the brand and dealership management system names in the posting. [O*NET's profile for automotive service technicians and mechanics](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/49-3023.00) has the full task list.

The highest-risk lines are the ones a dealership verifies before an offer: an ASE area you have not passed, a lapsed certification written as current, a factory level you did not reach, or production numbers your last shop will not confirm. Write what your certificates and your pay stubs show.
