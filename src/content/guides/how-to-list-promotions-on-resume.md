A promotion is the best evidence a resume can carry: another employer already bet on you and won. But most resumes bury it — two titles mashed into one entry, or split into what looks like two separate jobs. Either way, the reader misses the story. Here's how to make the climb impossible to miss.

## How to list promotions on a resume: the two-second rule

Whoever reads your resume is skimming. If your promotion takes detective work to spot, it doesn't exist. Every format below serves that one goal: company once, titles stacked newest-first, dates on each title.

## One promotion at the same company: stack the titles

Put the company on one line with your total years there, then each title with its own dates underneath.

**Before (the promotion is invisible):**

> Shift Supervisor, Danner Logistics — 2021–2026

**After (the climb is the headline):**

> **Danner Logistics** — 2021–2026
> *Shift Supervisor* (2024–2026)
> *Warehouse Associate* (2021–2024)

Same job history, but now the reader sees three things at once: loyalty, growth, and a company that promoted you.

## Multiple promotions: same ladder, more rungs

The stacked format scales. List every title, newest first, each with dates. Give your bullets to the most recent role, with one or two under earlier titles if they show something the current role doesn't. You don't need to repeat duties that carried upward — the reader assumes them.

## Bullets that prove it was a promotion

"Promoted" is a claim; the bullet underneath is the evidence. Say what grew:

> Promoted to Shift Supervisor after two years — now responsible for a crew of 9 and the site's most error-prone dock.

Scope is the language of promotions: more people, more money, more risk handed to you. If you can put an honest number on what expanded, do it.

## Title changed but the work didn't

Reorgs rename people without promoting them. Don't dress a re-title up as a climb — an interviewer will find the seam in one question. List the newer title and note the earlier one in plain text ("previously titled Line Lead") if the older name helps a keyword match.

## Promoted in duties but not in title

Common and frustrating: you absorbed the bigger job, the title never followed. Your resume can say exactly what's true — "took over scheduling and vendor orders for the department (responsibilities of the lead role)" — without inventing a title you never held. The scope is real; claim the scope, not the rank.

## Will an ATS read stacked titles correctly?

Mostly yes — if you keep the ladder machine-simple:

- **Each title on its own line, with its own dates.** That's the pattern parsers are built to recognize. Don't put the ladder in a table, columns, or a text box — those are where parsing breaks.
- **When the application form asks you to enter jobs one at a time, give each title its own entry there.** The form is its own parser; your document's clever formatting doesn't carry into it. Two titles = two entries in the form, even though your resume document stays stacked.
- **If a posting runs through an older system and you're nervous,** the safe fallback is repeating the company name above each title. It costs a little elegance and never costs parsing.

For what these systems actually do with your resume, see [what the ATS reads](/guides/what-the-ats-reads).

## The two mistakes that erase promotions

- **Splitting one company into two entries.** It reads as two short stints — job-hopping — instead of one climb. Worst possible trade.
- **Only listing the final title.** Clean, but it deletes the single strongest fact: they promoted you. Show the rung you started on.
