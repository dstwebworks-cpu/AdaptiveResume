HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) hiring starts with a question the law answers: can this person legally handle refrigerants? That makes an HVAC technician resume unusual — there's one credential a screener looks for before anything else, and a resume that buries it or states it vaguely loses ground to weaker techs who wrote it clearly. After that, a shop is reading for your real scope: service or install, residential or light commercial, which equipment, how many calls.

There's also a moment happening in this trade that your resume should catch. The industry is mid-way through a refrigerant transition, and a tech who can truthfully say "I'm trained on the new refrigerants" is answering a question shops are actively asking. Vague resumes miss all of this. Specific ones read like the tech a dispatcher wants on the board.

## Lead with your scope and the systems you run

Under each employer, give the picture in one line before the bullets — service or install, the equipment, the volume, and the credential.

**Before:** "Responsible for servicing and repairing residential HVAC systems."

**After:** "Residential service and repair, [6–8] calls a day across gas furnaces, heat pumps, and split systems; diagnosed refrigerant, airflow, and low-voltage control faults; [90]% first-visit completion; EPA 608 Universal."

## EPA 608 — the credential the law requires

Section 608 of the Clean Air Act, administered by the EPA (Environmental Protection Agency), requires technician certification for anyone who maintains, services, repairs, or disposes of equipment that could release refrigerants. It's the legal floor for the core of the job, and the first hard filter on an HVAC posting.

State it exactly, because the type tells a shop what you can touch:

- **Type I** — small appliances (five pounds of refrigerant or less)
- **Type II** — high-pressure appliances — the split systems and heat pumps that fill a service tech's day
- **Type III** — low-pressure appliances, chiller territory
- **Universal** — all of the above

"EPA 608 Universal" is one of the strongest four-word lines an HVAC resume can carry. If you hold a type rather than Universal, name the type — it's still the legal credential, and precision reads better than vagueness.

## NATE, HVAC Excellence, and state licensing — exactly as held

**NATE** (North American Technician Excellence) is a voluntary certification shops widely recognize. List the real credential you earned: the traditional path is a core exam plus a specialty in an installation or service track — air conditioning, air distribution, heat pumps, gas heating, and others — and the newer **Certified HVAC Professional (CHP-5)** pathway certifies through five shorter exams. NATE also offers a Low-GWP Refrigerant certification exam — worth a line if you've taken it.

**HVAC Excellence** runs its own recognized ladder — Employment Ready certificates for students and entry-level techs, Professional Technician certifications for experienced ones. List the level and subject you earned, not just the brand name.

**State licensing varies by state, and honesty here means saying so.** Some states license HVAC contractors, some license journeyman and master mechanics, some leave it to cities and counties — no single license is universal across the country. Write the one you actually hold with its issuing state and real title: "[State] journeyman HVAC license, #[number]." If your state doesn't license the work at your level, you lose nothing by not inventing one.

**OSHA cards:** an OSHA 10 or OSHA 30 card from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration's Outreach Training Program is worth a line. Write it as a card — "OSHA 30 card" — never as a certification, because the Outreach Program isn't one and a careful reader knows the difference.

## The refrigerant transition — say where you stand

The transition belongs on your resume because shops are hiring around it. Under the AIM Act (the American Innovation and Manufacturing Act), HFC refrigerants are being phased down, and new residential and light-commercial equipment manufactured since the start of 2025 has moved from R-410A to lower-GWP (global warming potential) refrigerants — mainly **R-454B** and **R-32**. Both are classified **A2L**, meaning mildly flammable, and they carry different handling, leak-detection, recovery, and charging practices than R-410A.

If you've trained on A2L refrigerants or installed and serviced R-454B or R-32 equipment, write it — it's current, specific, and scarce. And your R-410A and R-22 service history still matters, because the installed base runs for decades. "Service across R-22, R-410A, and A2L (R-454B) systems; A2L handling trained" tells a shop you span the whole installed base. Claim only the refrigerants you've actually put gauges on.

## HVAC resume skills that carry real weight

The phrase "HVAC experience" carries almost no information. These are the skills shops actually read for — name the ones that are truly yours:

- **Refrigeration circuit diagnosis** — superheat and subcooling, metering devices, compressor and reversing-valve faults. The heart of service work; say it in those words.
- **Electrical and low-voltage controls** — 24-volt control circuits, thermostats, control boards, capacitors, contactors, multimeter diagnosis. A large share of "no cool" calls end here.
- **Brazing** — line sets and fittings, with a nitrogen purge if that's your practice.
- **Airflow and ductwork** — static pressure readings, duct repair and sealing, and duct design to **ACCA Manual D** (ACCA is the Air Conditioning Contractors of America) if you've done design, not just installation.
- **Load calculation** — **ACCA Manual J** residential load calculations, if you perform them. A real dividing line between techs who size by rule of thumb and techs who calculate; claim it only if you run the numbers.
- **Heat pumps and mini-splits** — the growth end of the trade: cold-climate heat pumps, ducted and ductless mini-splits, multi-zone. If this is your strength, put it high.
- **Combustion and gas heat** — furnace diagnosis, combustion analysis if you carry and use an analyzer, gas piping, venting.
- **Preventive maintenance** — tune-ups and maintenance-agreement work: steady revenue for a shop, steady proof of thoroughness for you.

## Honest service metrics — the numbers a shop can feel

Service managers live in dispatch software, so numbers from it translate instantly. If your shop ran ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, FieldEdge, or similar, you may already know these: calls per day, **first-visit completion rate** (truck rolls saved — the number managers watch closest), **callback rate** (low is the badge; claim it only if you know it), maintenance agreements serviced, and systems installed per week for install work. "Ran [6–8] service calls a day with a [<5]% callback rate" is a line a service manager can feel in their own numbers. A metric you can't back up in conversation is worse than no metric — bracket what varies and write only what's true.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** Many HVAC resumes are one strong page, and if you've held one or two roles, that's the right call — but keep the specific proof. Your EPA 608 type, refrigerants handled, equipment mix, and real numbers are what a screener needs to find, so cut generic duty lines ("provided excellent customer service") before you cut a real credential or metric. If you've run service and install across several shops, **two pages is normal once you have more than one substantive role to describe** — a tight two pages beats a padded one.

One genuine exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change. That's a hard requirement, not a style preference.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, a clearly labeled certifications block near the top — EPA 608 type first — and skip the graphics. A screener wants your credential and equipment list fast, not a layout to admire.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Ran [6–8] residential service calls daily across [furnaces / heat pumps / split systems]; [90]% first-visit completion"
- "Hold EPA 608 [Type II / Universal] certification; serviced [R-22 / R-410A / R-454B] systems"
- "Completed A2L refrigerant training; installed and charged [R-454B / R-32] equipment"
- "Diagnosed low-voltage control, capacitor, and control-board faults with multimeter; repaired on first visit"
- "Brazed line sets with nitrogen purge on [N] installs; passed [N] inspections without correction"
- "Installed [N] systems per week: [equipment types], [1.5–5]-ton range, startup and commissioning included"
- "Serviced [N] maintenance agreements per season; performed combustion analysis on gas heat tune-ups"
- "Performed ACCA Manual J load calculations and Manual D duct sizing on [replacement / new-construction] jobs"

## What screening software looks for on an HVAC technician resume

Filters commonly read for: HVAC, EPA 608, Universal, refrigerant, refrigerant recovery, R-410A, R-454B, R-32, A2L, heat pump, mini-split, ductless, split system, furnace, air conditioning, brazing, low-voltage, thermostat, controls, superheat, subcooling, troubleshooting, preventive maintenance, Manual J, load calculation, ductwork, NATE, OSHA 10, and service calls. [O*NET's profile for heating, air conditioning, and refrigeration mechanics and installers](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/49-9021.00) has the full task and tool list.

The highest-risk lines are the ones that overstate scope — a 608 type you don't hold, a refrigerant you've never recovered, or load calculations you've never actually run. Shops ride techs along on day one, so overstatement gets found fast. Accurate detail is both safer and stronger.
