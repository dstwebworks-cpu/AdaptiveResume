If you've applied to a job online, your resume almost certainly passed through an **applicant tracking system (ATS)** first. The term gets thrown around with a lot of fear attached. Here's what an ATS actually is, in plain English — and what it does and doesn't do to your resume.

## The simple definition

An ATS is the software a company uses to **manage job applications**. When you hit "submit" on a careers page, your resume usually lands in an ATS, not directly in a person's inbox. Popular systems include Workday, Greenhouse, Taleo, iCIMS, and Lever, but there are dozens.

Its core jobs are:

- **Parse** your resume — pull it apart into fields (name, contact, job titles, dates, skills, education).
- **Store** it in a searchable database.
- **Search & sort** — let recruiters filter and rank applicants by keywords, titles, or requirements.
- **Track** — follow each applicant's status through the hiring stages.

That's it. At its heart, an ATS is a filing-and-search system for resumes.

## Where your application actually goes

It helps to see the whole trip:

1. **You submit.** Your file uploads, and any form questions you answered ride along with it.
2. **The software parses.** Your resume becomes a structured profile — titles here, dates there, skills in their own field.
3. **Knockout questions apply, if the employer set any.** These are the explicit yes/no filters: "Are you authorized to work in the US?", "Do you hold an active CDL?" Answer no to a hard requirement and the application can be closed automatically. This is the only routine "automatic rejection" — and it's driven by your form answers, not by a robot judging your resume's style.
4. **A human works the queue.** The recruiter opens the ATS, searches and filters the applicant pool, skims profiles and attached resumes, and moves people forward or not.

Notice what's missing from that sequence: no step where software grades your resume's design, and — at most employers — no step where an algorithm decides your worth. The software files; people choose.

## What an ATS does to *your* resume

The part that matters to you is the **parse**. The software reads your file and tries to drop your information into the right fields. If your resume is laid out cleanly, it parses cleanly — your titles, dates, and skills land where they should, and you show up correctly when a recruiter searches. If it's laid out in a way the parser can't follow (tables, text boxes, graphics, contact info in the header), pieces can get garbled or dropped — and you may not come up in searches you'd actually match.

The step-by-step of what survives a parse and what doesn't is in our companion guide, [what screening software actually reads](/guides/what-the-ats-reads).

## What recruiters do inside the ATS

Understanding the reader helps you write for them. A recruiter with two hundred applicants doesn't read two hundred resumes top to bottom. Inside the ATS they typically:

- **Search keywords** — a skill, a certification, a tool ("forklift," "OSHA 30," "NetSuite").
- **Filter** — by location, most recent title, or the knockout answers.
- **Skim the parsed profile first** — titles and dates in a uniform layout — and open your actual resume file when something matches.

That's why the words on your page matter more than the design: the search happens on the extracted text. It's also why [matching the job description's real vocabulary — honestly](/guides/honest-keyword-matching) — is the highest-value tailoring you can do.

## The myth worth clearing up

You've probably seen the claim that an ATS "automatically rejects 75% of resumes before a human ever sees them." That stat is repeated everywhere and is **largely unsubstantiated** — it doesn't trace to solid evidence, and we won't repeat it as fact. The reality is more mundane: most systems **store and organize** applications; humans still do the reviewing. Automatic knockouts exist, but usually only for **explicit, employer-set filters** (e.g., "must have an active CDL," "must be authorized to work in the US") — not some robot tossing resumes on a whim.

So the goal isn't to "trick the robot." It's simpler: make sure the software can **read your resume cleanly** and that the real keywords for your experience are actually on the page.

## What an ATS is NOT

Worth stating plainly, because the fear-marketing version of the ATS sells a lot of gimmicks:

- **It isn't a taste critic.** No common system scores your font choices or rewards a flashier template. Design exists for the human reader — and even for them, clear beats clever.
- **It isn't secretly reading between the lines.** It extracts what's written. If a skill isn't on the page in words, it doesn't exist to the search.
- **It isn't beatable by tricks.** Hidden white-text keywords and stuffed skill lists may make it into the database — and then a person opens the file. Every trick is visible to the human it was meant to fool.

## Four terms, translated

- **Parse** — the software reading your file into structured fields.
- **Knockout question** — an explicit yes/no requirement set by the employer; the one true auto-reject.
- **Keyword search** — how recruiters find candidates in the pool; it runs on your resume's actual words.
- **Ranking** — some systems order candidates by how well extracted terms match the posting. A ranking is a sort order for the recruiter's attention, not a verdict.

## What this means for you

Two practical things:

1. **Format for a clean parse** — single column, standard headings, real text (not an image), contact info in the body. The [formatting guide](/guides/resume-format-for-ats) covers every decision.
2. **Use the real words** — mirror the job description's terminology *only* where it's genuinely true of you. Never paste in keywords for skills you don't have; that gets caught by the human a few minutes later.

> No honest tool can promise you'll "pass" a specific company's ATS — that depends on their settings and keywords, which you can't see. What you *can* control is whether your resume reads cleanly and tells the truth well.
