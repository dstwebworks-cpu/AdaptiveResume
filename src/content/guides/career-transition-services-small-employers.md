When a company of 30 people lets 6 of them go, the owner usually finds out that career transition services are priced for a company of 3,000. The published per-person packages, the account teams, and the coaching hours were built for the layoff that makes the news, not the one that happens quietly on a Friday in a small office.

This guide is for the HR lead or owner who wants to do right by the people leaving without buying a program sized for someone else. It explains what career transition services actually contain, what each piece is for, and how to decide whether you need the whole package or a smaller, honest piece of it.

## What career transition services contain

A full career transition program, which the vendors still call outplacement, is usually a bundle of five things:

1. **A resume.** Either the person's resume rewritten by a writer, or a template and a coach who reviews what the person writes.
2. **Coaching.** A set number of sessions, by phone or video, on job-search strategy and interviews.
3. **A job-search platform.** A login with job listings, networking tools, and sometimes practice interview software.
4. **Administrative support.** Someone who checks in, tracks who has used the service, and reports usage back to you.
5. **A report to the employer.** Utilization figures, and sometimes placement figures, so the company can show the program was delivered.

One large provider's published rate card, as of 2026, lists a resume critique at about $900 per person and a package with a custom-written resume and coaching at about $1,900 to $2,600 per person. Another large provider's published card starts at $2,400 per person. Most providers do not publish prices at all and quote each employer separately.

## What each piece is worth to a small employer

The honest way to size a program is to ask what each of the five pieces does for your people, not what the bundle costs.

**The resume is the one piece every departing employee uses.** Coaching sessions often go unused once people are busy interviewing, and many people open the platform login once. We have not seen a published breakdown by component, so treat that as experience, not data. The resume is what they send out that week. The best available figure suggests that fewer than half of the employees offered a full outplacement program ever use it, and that figure is old enough that we treat it as a caution rather than a fact. Whatever the true number is, the resume is the part that gets used.

**Coaching matters most for people who have not searched for a job in a decade.** A 22-year employee who was promoted three times and never wrote a resume for any of it gets real value from a coach. A 3-year employee who was job-searching last year does not need the same thing.

**The platform and the reporting exist for the employer's benefit,** so the company can document that help was offered. That is a legitimate reason to buy them. It is also a reason to be honest with yourself about who the money is for.

For a 5-to-50-person layoff, this usually means one of two shapes. Either you buy the full program for a few long-tenured people and a resume-only service for everyone else, or you buy a resume-only service for everyone and offer coaching to the few who ask for it.

## What "resumes done right" means

A resume-only transition service is only worth buying if the resume is genuinely better than what the person would have written alone. Reformatting the old resume into a new template does not meet that bar. Here is what does.

**The service works backwards from what the person actually did.** People who have been in a role for years stop noticing what they know. The machines they run, the standards they work under, the people they trained, and the systems they use every day never made it onto the page because they became routine.

**Every suggestion is a question.** The person sees each proposed line and says yes, edits it, or skips it. Nothing lands on the page they did not confirm.

**Credentials are handled with care.** A license or certification is the highest-risk line on any resume. It is never assumed from a job title; it is asked about, and anything uncertain waits for a reviewer.

**The finished file reads cleanly to screening software.** A single column, standard headings, and no graphics, so the resume does not come out scrambled at the first employer that runs it through an applicant tracking system.

Here is what that looks like for one departing employee. This is a sample worker, not a real person.

**Before:** "Warehouse lead. Responsible for daily operations and helping the team with whatever came up."

**After:** "Warehouse lead for a 14-person day shift: scheduled the shift, ran daily cycle counts in NetSuite, trained 9 new hires on forklift safety under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.178, and kept the receiving dock at zero safety incidents for 3 years."

Nothing in the second version was invented. The shift size, the system, the training, and the safety record were all real. The person had simply never written them down, and a reformatted version of the first line would still say nothing.

## How to decide, in four questions

Ask these before you take a quote from anyone, including us.

1. **How many of the people leaving have been here more than ten years?** Those are the people a coach helps most. Everyone else needs a strong resume first.
2. **Does the severance agreement promise a specific service?** If it names a program, buy that program. If it says "career transition assistance," ask your attorney whether a resume-only service satisfies the wording before you rely on it.
3. **Do you need a usage report?** If the answer is yes, ask every provider what they report and how. A resume service should be able to show you who completed a resume, without promising anyone a job.
4. **What will each person actually use?** If you cannot picture a departing employee logging into a job-search platform, do not pay for the platform.

## A bullet bank for departing employees. Keep only what is true.

These are lines an employee can adapt. Each bracket is a fact only that person can fill in.

- Led a [N]-person [team or shift]: scheduling, daily [system] records, and [task].
- Trained [N] new hires on [procedure or system].
- Ran [process] to [standard, for example ISO 9001 or OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147], with [result].
- Handled [N] customer accounts with [result] over [N] months.
- Moved [process] from [old method] to [new system], which [measurable change].
- Held [license or certification], renewed [year].

## Format notes for the resumes your program produces

Two pages is normal once a person has more than one substantive role, and a 20-year employee should not be forced onto one page. Reverse chronological order, a single column, and no graphics or photos. If anyone in your group is applying for federal jobs, applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages as of the September 2025 OPM change, and a federal resume asks for details a private-sector one leaves off.

For the notice side of a layoff, the U.S. Department of Labor's [WARN Act page](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/layoffs/warn) explains which employers are covered and what the 60-day notice requires. Notice and transition help are separate obligations; this guide is about the second one.
