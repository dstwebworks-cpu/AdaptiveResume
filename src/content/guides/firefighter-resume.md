Fire service hiring is a certification-block profession. Before anyone reads a bullet, a screener — human or software — checks the block: Firefighter I/II, EMT level, HazMat, driver/operator. But the block only opens the door. What separates candidates at the interview panel is the shift record behind the certs: the calls run, the apparatus operated, the station work owned. A firefighter resume needs both halves, each stated exactly.

## What hiring managers and screening software look for on a firefighter resume

The vocabulary that matters, which is to say the vocabulary of what the job typically involves:

- **Fire certifications:** Firefighter I and II (the NFPA 1001-based standard most states certify against), listed with the certifying body and dates. HazMat Awareness and Operations levels. Driver/operator or apparatus engineer qualifications where held.
- **The EMS half:** EMT-Basic, AEMT, or Paramedic, with current-through dates. Many departments run medical calls as a core part of the workload, and the EMS credential is often part of the screening filter itself.
- **Incident command basics:** ICS-100, ICS-200, and NIMS IS-700 completions — routinely required and easily listed exactly.
- **The work:** structure and vehicle fire response, SCBA operations, vehicle extrication, ladder and hose evolutions, pump operations, search, salvage and overhaul, fire prevention and public education, station and equipment readiness.
- **Physical and process gates:** CPAT completion where part of your record; wildland quals (S-130/S-190, red card) if you carry them.

[O*NET's Firefighters profile (33-2011.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/33-2011.00) holds the occupation's base vocabulary; your department specifics and qualifications carry the rest.

## The EMS half of the job

If your department responds to medical calls, your resume should read like it. That means the EMS credential in the certification block, and at least one bullet that shows medical work as part of your normal shift: patient assessment and care at your certification level, working under local protocols, handoffs to transporting units or hospital staff. Candidates sometimes bury the EMS half because the fire half feels like the identity. Screeners read it the other way: a firefighter who documents both halves matches more of the posting.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Firefighter[/EMT or /Paramedic], [department], [career/volunteer], [shift schedule], [years]"
- "Responded to structure fires, vehicle fires, collisions, and medical calls across [district/coverage area]"
- "Operated as interior attack, search, and ventilation crew member under SCBA"
- "Qualified driver/operator on [engine/ladder]; pump operations at fire scenes"
- "Performed vehicle extrication with [hydraulic tools]"
- "Provided patient care at the [EMT/AEMT/Paramedic] level under [county/agency] protocols"
- "Completed daily apparatus, SCBA, and equipment checks; documented readiness"
- "Conducted fire prevention activities: [inspections, hydrant testing, public education, school visits]"
- "Completed ICS-100, ICS-200, and NIMS IS-700"
- "Maintained [station duties/training hours] per department requirements"

Fill the brackets with your department's reality. Certification records get checked directly in fire hiring processes, so the block and the bullets must match what the certifying body would confirm.

## How long should a firefighter resume be for a hiring process?

One page for most candidates; two only when a long career, officer record, or instructor history genuinely needs it. Structure: certification block near the top with certifying bodies and current-through dates, then experience with a department line (department, role, career or volunteer, shift, years) atop each entry, then education and physical-ability completions. Single column, standard headings, no graphics. If you're applying through a civil-service process with its own application form, the resume still matters — it's what the interview panel holds while you talk.

## Volunteer, part-time, and wildland time all count

List every kind of fire service honestly labeled: volunteer departments with real call volume, part-time or paid-on-call work, seasonal wildland assignments with crew type and quals. The fire service is one of the few fields where unpaid experience is fully respected — provided the label is exact. "Volunteer firefighter" presented as career time is the kind of inflation a background check surfaces immediately; "Volunteer firefighter, [department], [N] years, [call types]" is a record departments hire from every year.

## Promoting up: driver/operator and company officer

If you're testing for driver/operator, engineer, or lieutenant, the resume the panel holds should already read one step up: apparatus qualifications with dates, pump operations at working fires, any acting-officer time labeled exactly as acting, training you've delivered at the station, and instructor certifications if held. Promotion panels know your department; the resume that helps you is the one that organizes your record cleanly, not the one that stretches it.
