The hardest part of a career change isn't the new field — it's getting a resume past someone who only sees your old one. The instinct is to hide where you came from. The better move is to **translate** what you already did into what the new role needs.

## Separate the skill from the setting

Most of what you do is more portable than your job title suggests. A restaurant shift lead runs scheduling, inventory, training, and conflict resolution under pressure — that's operations management, just in an apron. Start by listing what you *did*, stripped of the industry it happened in.

## The inventory method: three columns

The most reliable way to find your transferable skills is mechanical, not inspirational. Take a page and make three columns:

1. **What I actually did** — concrete tasks from each role, in your own words. "Made the weekly schedule for 14 people." "Handled the angry ones nobody else would take." "Counted the safe every night."
2. **The skill that names it** — the portable, industry-neutral term: workforce scheduling, escalation handling, cash controls.
3. **Where the new field uses it** — pull a real job posting from the target field and find where that skill appears in *their* words.

Column one is your history. Column three is your resume. Column two is the bridge — and the honest test is that every entry in column two must trace back to something real in column one. The **Transferable-Skills Inventory** worksheet below is this exercise, laid out and ready.

## Map transferable skills to the career change resume you're writing

Pull the job description for the role you want. For each requirement, ask: "where have I genuinely done this, even if it was called something else?" Match honestly — if you've done a near-version, describe the near-version; don't claim the exact one.

- Customer escalations → stakeholder management
- Closing the till / reconciling drawers → financial accuracy and reporting
- Training new hires → onboarding and enablement

This is the same honest keyword discipline that [gets a resume found in screening software](/guides/honest-keyword-matching) — the new field's vocabulary, applied only where it's true of you.

## One example of the separation

A restaurant shift manager moving toward operations coordination writes: *"Managed restaurant shifts."*

Separated from its setting, the same experience reads: *"Scheduled and led teams of 12-15 across peak periods · managed vendor deliveries and inventory against a weekly budget · handled escalations and kept service running through equipment failures and no-shows."*

Nothing about restaurants disappeared — but every line now names a skill the target role uses daily. That's the whole move: same true experience, framed by the skill instead of the setting.

## The skills that transfer highest

Different fields, same machinery underneath. If your history includes any of these, they belong in column two — nearly every industry buys them:

- **People:** supervising, scheduling, training, de-escalation
- **Money:** budgets, cash handling, cost control, vendor negotiation
- **Process:** safety and compliance, quality checks, documentation, inventory
- **Pressure:** deadline work, incident response, coverage when things break
- **Communication:** customer-facing work, reporting upward, coordinating across teams

The mistake career changers make isn't lacking these — it's leaving them buried under an industry-specific job title.

## Chronological, functional, or hybrid — settled honestly

Career-change advice often pushes the "functional" format: skills up top, work history reduced to a bare list. Skip it. Recruiters have seen that format used to bury things for decades, and it reads as concealment even when nothing's hidden — some screening software also parses it badly, [scrambling your history in the database](/guides/what-the-ats-reads).

What works is a **chronological resume with a skills-forward opening**: a summary that frames the through-line, a compact core-skills block carrying your transferable vocabulary, then your real work history — reframed bullet by bullet, dates intact, nothing hidden. You get the reframing benefit of a functional resume with none of its credibility cost.

## Lead with a skills-based summary

Career changers benefit from a short summary up top that frames the through-line ("operations and people leadership across [old field], now focused on [new field]"). It tells the reader how to read everything below.

## Use accomplishments, not titles, to carry the weight

Your old titles may mean nothing in the new field; your results travel. [Quantify truthfully](/guides/quantify-resume-bullets) — what you improved, ran, or saved — so the reader judges capability, not pedigree.

## Address the change, don't hide it

A one-line note ("transitioning into [field] after [N] years building [transferable strength]") reads as intentional, not confused. Confidence about the change beats pretending it isn't happening.

## Stay honest about the gap

Transferable doesn't mean identical. If the new role needs a skill you don't have, name it as a growth area or get the credential — don't paper over it. That honesty is exactly what survives a reference check.
