Every state runs its own unemployment insurance program, so the first step is finding the right state and its official site. The table below links the unemployment page for all 50 states and the District of Columbia. We opened every link on 11/03/2026 to make sure it goes to the state agency itself.

This page explains how filing works in general. Each state sets its own rules, amounts and deadlines, so your state's site is the final word on your claim.

## How to file for unemployment, step by step

**1. Find the right state.** The U.S. Department of Labor's [unemployment insurance fact sheet](https://oui.doleta.gov/unemploy/uifactsheet.asp) says you should generally file with the state where you worked. If you worked in a different state from where you live now, or in several states, the unemployment agency in the state where you now live can tell you how to file with the others.

**2. Check whether you may qualify.** According to the [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/unemployment-insurance), you generally must be unemployed through no fault of your own. In most states, that means you left your last job because of a lack of available work, which is what a layoff is. You also must meet your state's requirements for wages earned or time worked during a "base period." In most states, that is the first four of the last five completed calendar quarters before you file. Your state decides whether you qualify, so file even if you are unsure.

**3. File as soon as possible.** The Department says to contact your state's program as soon as possible after becoming unemployed, and that the first payment generally arrives two to three weeks after you file. Your state's site lists what to have ready and whether you can file online, by phone, or both.

**4. Keep your claim going.** After the first claim, the Department of Labor says you must file weekly or biweekly claims after each week ends and answer questions about whether you are still eligible. Your state may also ask you to record the employers you contact while you look for work. Our free weekly claim and work-search log at the bottom of this page gives you one place to write both down.

**One tax decision to make now.** Unemployment benefits count as income. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc418) says you generally must include them on your tax return, and you can choose to have federal income tax withheld by filing Form W-4V.

**Former federal employees and veterans.** The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/unemployment-insurance) lists separate programs for former civilian federal employees (Unemployment Compensation for Federal Employees) and for ex-military personnel (Unemployment Compensation for Ex-Service Members). If either applies to you, ask the agency in your state about it when you file.

## Find your nearest American Job Center

American Job Centers are the public offices that help people find work, and there are about 2,300 of them across the country. The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/american-job-centers) says they offer job search help and re-employment services for people on unemployment, and that services vary by state. Some services have eligibility rules, so ask your local center what it offers.

To find a center near you, use the [American Job Center Finder](https://www.careeronestop.org/LocalHelp/AmericanJobCenters/find-american-job-centers.aspx) on CareerOneStop, or call the Department of Labor's toll-free help line at 1-877-US-2JOBS. The same line can answer general questions about job loss. If retraining is on your mind, ask about the WIOA Dislocated Worker program. The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/workforce-investment/adult) says it is designed to help workers get back to work as quickly as possible, through American Job Centers, and our guide for [people out of work six months or more](/guides/long-term-unemployed-restarting-your-job-search/) explains who qualifies and how training is paid for.
