You put real hours into earning a certification — study nights, an exam fee, maybe a whole renewal cycle. Then it ends up buried in a skills line where nobody sees it, or worded so loosely it sounds like a claim you can't back. Here's exactly where certifications go on a resume, what order to put them in, how to format each line, and how to handle the messy cases — in progress, expired, or actually a license — without overstating a single one.

## Where to list certifications on a resume

Give them their own labeled section: **Certifications** (or **Licenses & Certifications** if you hold both). Screening software keys on standard section headings, and a labeled block is what recruiters' eyes jump to when a posting requires a credential.

Placement depends on how load-bearing the cert is:

- **Required for the job** (nursing, IT, project management, skilled trades): put the section high — right after your summary, before or right after Experience.
- **Supporting evidence**: after Experience and Education is fine.
- **After your name** — only for credentials your field routinely puts there: RN, PE, CPA, PMP. A useful test: if job postings in your field put the acronym in the job title, it can follow your name. If not, keep it in the section.

## What order to list certifications on a resume

Not alphabetical, and not oldest-first. Two rules, in order:

1. **Relevance first.** The credential the posting asks for leads the block, every time — even if it's your oldest.
2. **Then recency.** Among certs of similar relevance, newest first, because currency is part of what a credential says.

If you hold many, trim rather than stack: three relevant lines beat eight mixed ones, and the trimmed ones can live on your long-form profile instead.

## The format line: full name, acronym, issuer, date

One line per credential, in this shape:

**Full name (Acronym) — Issuing organization, date earned or current-through date**

- Certified ScrumMaster (CSM) — Scrum Alliance, earned 2025
- CompTIA Security+ — CompTIA, current through 05/2028
- OSHA 30-Hour General Industry — completed 2024

Spell out the full name *and* the acronym, because you can't know which one a recruiter searches for. Name the issuer — "Six Sigma Green Belt" means different things from different providers, and the issuer is what makes the line checkable. Include the expiration or current-through date whenever the credential has one; an unlabeled expiring cert invites the question you least want asked in an interview.

## In-progress certifications: the honest version

A cert you're actively pursuing can absolutely go on the page — labeled as exactly what it is:

- **PMP — exam scheduled [month/year]** (when a date is booked)
- **AWS Solutions Architect Associate — in progress, expected [month/year]** (when you're partway through)

What never works: listing the bare acronym under Certifications and letting the reader assume it's earned. Credentials get checked — during background checks, credentialing, or a casual interview question — and "I meant I was planning to take it" is a conversation you don't recover from. If you haven't started studying and have no date, it doesn't belong on the resume at all.

## Expired certifications

Three honest options:

1. **Renew before you apply**, if it's required and renewable — the cleanest fix.
2. **List it as expired** — "CCNA — Cisco (expired 2023)" — when the underlying skill still matters and you're upfront about status.
3. **Leave it off** when it's neither current nor relevant.

The one option that doesn't exist: listing it as if it were current. That's a dated, checkable claim.

## Licenses vs. certifications

They're not the same thing, and readers in licensed fields know the difference. A **license** is government permission to practice — a state nursing license, an electrician's license, a CDL. A **certification** is a credential from a professional body. If you hold both, use the heading **Licenses & Certifications** and put the license first with its state and status: "Registered Nurse — Georgia, active." Employers in licensed fields screen on the license before anything else.

## The mistakes that cost interviews

- Acronym only, no full name — invisible to half the searches.
- "Certified in project management" with no credential name or issuer — reads as a claim, not a credential.
- An in-progress cert formatted like an earned one.
- The required cert buried at the bottom of page two.
- Course completions dressed up as certifications — a weekend course is worth listing, but call it what it is, under Training or Professional Development.

## A before-and-after

![Before and after example of an improved resume line - every line confirmed by the person](/img/guides/examples/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume-example.png)

*Illustrative example. On your resume, every line comes from your real history - proposed as a question, added only when you confirm it.*

**Before:** "Certified in project management and additional IT certifications."

**After:** "Project Management Professional (PMP) — Project Management Institute, current through 09/2027 · CompTIA A+ — CompTIA, earned 2024."

Same person, same credentials — no new facts added. The rewrite just names what was already true precisely enough to be searched, checked, and believed. That's the whole job of this section: every line should survive the question "can I see the credential?"
