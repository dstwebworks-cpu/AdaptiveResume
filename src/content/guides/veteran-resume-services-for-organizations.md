The organizations that help veterans find civilian work are short on one thing, and it is not demand. One of the largest veteran employment nonprofits told Congress in June 2025 that it provides one-on-one employment services to about 25,000 transitioning service members, veterans, and military spouses a year. A case study of the same organization, using 2013 and 2014 data, estimated that about 113 registrants a week went without an assigned transition specialist. A federal audit found that about a quarter of service members who needed the most transition support skipped the mandatory two-day transition class, and 70 percent had not started the program a year out from separation.

Behind every one of those numbers is a resume that has to be written, and a counselor who does not have the hours to write it. This guide is for the program director, county veteran service officer, or SkillBridge partner who needs a per-person veteran resume service that can carry that load without inflating anyone's record.

## Why the veteran resume is the bottleneck

A veteran's resume is harder to write than a civilian's for three reasons, and all three land on the counselor's desk.

**The language does not transfer.** "LS2, guided-missile destroyer" and "supervised junior sailors" are true and mean nothing to a civilian hiring manager or the screening software in front of them. The work behind those lines, inventory control for thousands of line items and millions of dollars in parts, training a team, holding a clearance, has to be found and put in words the civilian side searches for.

**The scope is undersold.** Service members are trained not to boast, and a resume that says "squad leader" leaves out the head count, the equipment value, the inspection results, and the training delivered. A civilian manager reads scope in numbers, and the numbers are missing.

**The high-risk lines are the important ones.** A clearance, a military school that maps to a civilian credential, a commercial driver's license waiver. These are the lines that get a veteran an interview and the lines where a careless writer does the most damage. They have to be written exactly as the record shows them or not at all.

A counselor can do all of this for one veteran in an afternoon. A caseload of 200 does not have 200 afternoons.

## What a per-person veteran resume service must do

If you are buying this service for a program, or building it in-house, hold it to five requirements.

**1. Translate toward the civilian job, honestly.** Each MOS, rate, or AFSC is mapped to the nearest real civilian function, in the direction of the work the veteran is going for. A 92Y becomes inventory control and cycle counting, because that is what the work was. It does not become "supply chain manager" unless the person managed a supply chain.

**2. State scope in civilian numbers.** That means people led, dollar value signed for, vehicles maintained, readiness rates, and sailors trained. Numbers the veteran actually knew go in; a number nobody could check stays off the page.

**3. Write the clearance as held.** The line is the level, the status, and the date, with nothing from the classified side, no program names, and no project details. We never write "clearable," and we never write a lapsed clearance as active.

**4. Handle credentials by asking.** Many military schools map to civilian certifications, and the services' Credentialing Opportunities On-Line (COOL) sites list which ones. A credential the veteran actually earned goes on by its civilian name and date. Training completed without the credential is listed as training. A CDL earned through the military skills test waiver goes on once the state issues it, not before.

**5. Confirm every line with the veteran.** Each suggested line is a question. Nothing lands on the page the veteran did not confirm, and anything uncertain waits for your program's reviewer.

Here is what that produces for one veteran. This is a sample service member, not a real person.

**Before:** "LS2, guided-missile destroyer. Responsible for supply and inventory. Supervised junior sailors. Handled hazmat."

**After:** "Supply and inventory lead (Navy Logistics Specialist, E-5), guided-missile destroyer: 4,200 line items and $3.1M in parts across 6 storerooms. 99% inventory accuracy across 4 audits. Supervised and trained 7 sailors. Hazardous materials handling, Navy course completed 2023. Secret clearance, DoD: eligibility current, last held access 03/2026."

Nothing was added that was not already true. The line-item count, the dollar value, the audit results, the sailors trained, and the clearance date were all in the veteran's record. They just were not written down at their real size, or in words a civilian reader knows.

## Buying it as a program

Veteran-serving organizations buy services in a few shapes, and the per-person model fits all of them.

**County and state veteran service offices** usually have a small discretionary budget and a caseload that spikes around separation cycles. A per-person service lets the office buy exactly the number of resumes it needs in a quarter.

**SkillBridge industry partners** take service members in their last 180 days and owe them a resume that reflects both the military record and the SkillBridge work. Each participant gets a dated line for the program, the employer, and what they did there.

**Veteran-serving nonprofits** report cost per participant to funders. A per-person resume service is a clean line item with a known cost per participant.

**Workforce boards with a veteran caseload** already deliver resume assistance as a named service. A per-person vendor slots into the existing service, and the government's own published per-person rates for resume-specific deliverables, which run from about $100 for a basic resume to about $700 for a job-readiness package that includes a resume in the states that publish them, are a fair reference for what the service is worth.

## Format notes

Two pages is normal once a veteran has more than one substantive assignment. The most recent assignments in detail, the earlier ones as one line each with rank, role, and dates. Reverse chronological order, a single column, no graphics. For the federal track, applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages as of the September 2025 OPM change, and veterans' preference belongs on the federal application, never on the private-sector resume. The transition-program findings above come from the U.S. Government Accountability Office's report [GAO-23-106793](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106793).
