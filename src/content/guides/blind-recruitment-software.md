Blind recruitment software makes one change to a hiring process: at the screening step, the reviewer does not see who the candidate is. Names, photos, and contact details are hidden, sometimes school names and dates too, and the reviewer decides on experience and answers alone. The idea is old, and the study most often cited for it, Goldin and Rouse's 2000 paper on American symphony orchestras that adopted screened auditions, reported that hiding the musician from the jury was associated with more women advancing. That finding is suggestive rather than settled: the study's own estimates were imprecise, later commentators have questioned how strong the effect was, and a 2017 Australian government trial found that de-identifying applications slightly lowered the shortlisting of women and of minority candidates, the opposite of what the trial expected. Treat blind review as a reasonable control, not a proven cure.

The idea is sound. The software category built on it is real. What this guide adds is the part vendors say less about: what a blind review actually removes, what it cannot, and why the quality of the underlying record matters more than the masking. It ends with how a masked, line-by-line review of a confirmed skills record works when a staffing agency or an employer runs one.

## What blind review removes

At the screening step, a blind review removes the signals that are easiest to act on without noticing: a name that suggests a gender or an ethnicity, a photo, an address that suggests a neighborhood, an email address that suggests an age. Removing them does not make the reviewer fair. It removes a set of cues so that the decision has to rest on something else, and what it rests on is the record of experience in front of the reviewer.

That is the point most buyers miss. Blind review shifts all the weight onto the record. If the record is thin, the review is a coin flip with the name covered.

## What it cannot remove

Three things survive masking, and any honest vendor will tell you so.

**Signals inside the experience.** Where someone worked, how many years they have been working, which schools they attended if those are left visible, and the vocabulary they use to describe their work. These are proxies, and a reviewer can act on them without ever seeing a name.

**Thin records.** A candidate whose resume says "Warehouse associate, 4 years" is not helped by blind review. The reviewer has nothing to judge except the title. Candidates with the least practice at writing about their work are the ones a masked review serves worst, unless the record is built out first.

**The rest of the process.** The interview, the reference check, and the offer are not blind. A masked screen that advances a candidate into an unmasked interview has moved the decision, not removed it.

Whatever selection procedure you use, including a blind one, stays subject to the federal Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures at 29 CFR part 1607, which set the standard for whether a selection procedure has an adverse impact and how it must be validated if it does. Masking does not exempt a process from that analysis. Ban-the-box laws in many states and cities also govern when criminal history may be asked about, regardless of what the screen shows.

## The record is the review

If the record is what the reviewer judges, then the record has to be complete and true before masking helps anyone. Two things make that hard.

First, candidates under-describe their own work. Routine work becomes invisible to the person doing it, and long-tenured people write their job title where a paragraph belongs.

Second, records that were built to impress rather than to inform are worse under blind review, not better, because there is no name to lend them credibility. A masked reviewer reads "results-driven team player" and learns nothing.

The fix is to build the record before the review: work backwards through what the candidate actually did, put words to it, and have the candidate confirm every line. Here is the difference for one candidate. This is a sample, not a real person.

**Before, as submitted:** "Warehouse associate, 4 years. Forklift. Team player."

**After, confirmed line by line:** "Warehouse associate on receiving and put-away for 4 years at a regional food distributor; reach-truck and sit-down forklift certified, renewed 2025; about 400 pallets a shift in a SAP EWM environment; trained 6 new associates on receiving; zero recordable incidents on my shift over the last 2 years."

Now a masked reviewer has something to decide on. Every item was already the candidate's; nobody added a credential, and the forklift certification was asked about and confirmed, not assumed from the word "forklift."

## How a masked, line-by-line review works

This is how our review queue runs it for an agency or an employer. It is not a screening platform and does not replace one; it produces the record and lets your reviewer decide on it, masked or not.

1. **Intake.** The agency or employer sends up to three of the candidate's resumes and a LinkedIn export if the candidate provides one. During a pilot we handle intake under a signed data-processing agreement.
2. **The record is rebuilt.** Each likely skill, tool, standard, and credential becomes a proposed line with the reason it was proposed.
3. **Masking is a setting per organization.** With it on, the reviewer queue reduces the candidate's name to initials and hides the contact details, so the reviewer sees lines, not identities. It is set at intake, on request; the finished resume, the ZIP, and the audit report carry the candidate's name, because those documents leave the queue.
4. **Confirmation mode is a setting too.** In hybrid mode your reviewer decides every experience line, and anything that looks like a credential can be sent to the candidate through a link that needs no login, with one question: do you hold this? In records-only mode your reviewer confirms everything, credentials included, from the documents on file and records the basis.
5. **Every decision is recorded.** Confirmations, rejections, and credential holds land on an append-only audit trail, and each candidate gets a Review Audit report, a PDF of every item's journey with the sign-off line. That is the document you produce if anyone asks how a decision was made.
6. **Output.** A finished resume in DOCX and PDF per candidate, a ZIP of every signed-off candidate for the organization, and an organization report in CSV with each candidate's review status.

What this gives a blind process is a record worth reviewing blind, and a trail that shows the review happened on the lines.

## Format notes

For a masked review, put the experience first and keep identifying details in a header block that the queue can hide. One column, standard headings, no photos or graphics, so the same file reads cleanly to an applicant tracking system afterward. Dates stay; a review that hides tenure hides a fact the employer will see at the interview anyway. The federal selection guidelines are at [29 CFR part 1607](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XIV/part-1607).
