Plumber hiring runs on trust. The foreman or service manager reading your resume wants to know one thing: can they send you to rough in a building or run a service route, and trust the work to pass inspection and hold up without a callback (a return trip to fix your own work)? Generic language ("experienced plumber skilled in all aspects of residential and commercial plumbing") doesn't answer that. The specifics do — the license tier you hold, the code you build to, and the inspections you've passed.

The other thing that trips up plumber resumes is scope vagueness. Service plumbing, new-construction rough-in, remodel, and commercial work are related but different jobs, and a resume that just says "plumber" competes badly against one that names the license, the code, and the work.

## The scope line: your work in one sentence

Under each role, give one line another plumber would recognize instantly.

**Before:** "Plumber responsible for installing and repairing plumbing systems in residential and commercial buildings."

**After:** "Journeyman plumber, [state] license #[N]; roughed in DWV (drain-waste-vent) and PEX water supply for [N] units on a [4]-story multifamily build to the [IPC/UPC], set [N] fixtures and [N] water heaters, and passed [N] rough-in and final inspections with no re-inspections."

## Credentials, exactly as held

Every item in this block gets checked against the state licensing board, so it has to be exactly right. Our guide on [how to list certifications on a resume](/guides/how-to-list-certifications-on-resume/) covers placement and format; here is what typically belongs in a plumber block:

- **State license, at the tier you hold.** Plumbing licensing is set by each state, and in some states by the city or county. The tiers are typically apprentice, journeyman, and master, and the exam and experience requirements vary by jurisdiction. Write the state, the tier, and the number: "[State] master plumber license #[N], current through [year]." Never write "licensed plumber" without the state and tier — that's exactly what the reader will ask.
- **Apprenticeship, union or non-union.** The UA (United Association of Journeymen and Apprentices of the Plumbing and Pipe Fitting Industry of the United States and Canada) runs a five-year registered apprenticeship through its locals that tops out as a journeyman plumber; list your local and year: "UA Local [N], plumber apprentice, year [N] of 5." On the non-union side, PHCC (the Plumbing-Heating-Cooling Contractors Association) offers PHCC Academy, the PHCC Educational Foundation's online apprentice courses, approved state by state through PHCC chapters, and ABC (Associated Builders and Contractors) chapters in many areas run registered apprenticeships that include plumbing. Name the one that trained you.
- **NCCER Plumbing.** NCCER (National Center for Construction Education and Research) publishes a four-level Plumbing curriculum — from tools, materials, and pipe types in Level 1 through service plumbing, codes, and medical gas in the later levels. List the highest level you completed: "NCCER Plumbing, Level [N]."
- **Backflow prevention assembly tester.** The accepted tester credential varies by state and water purveyor. The ASSE International standard for testers is **ASSE 5110** (Backflow Prevention Assembly Tester), part of the ASSE Series 5000 cross-connection control qualifications. Write what you hold and who issued it: "Backflow prevention assembly tester, ASSE 5110, [certifying body], current through [year]."
- **ASSE 6010 Medical Gas Systems Installer**, from ASSE International, where you install medical gas and vacuum piping in hospitals and clinics. Only list it if you hold it, with the date.
- **OSHA 10-hour or 30-hour Construction.** These are course-completion cards from the OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) Outreach Training Program. OSHA itself says the cards are not a certification, so list them as "OSHA 30-Hour Construction, [year]" — never as "OSHA certified."
- **EPA Section 608 technician certification**, only if you also service equipment that holds refrigerant — HVAC or refrigeration work. The EPA (Environmental Protection Agency) requires it for technicians who maintain, service, repair, or dispose of that equipment. If you're strictly a plumber, leave it off; it isn't a plumbing credential.

List exactly what you hold, with real dates. A tier you haven't tested for or an expired backflow certification written as current is the fastest way to lose ground in a trade where the license is checked before you're on the payroll.

## The parts of the job that carry the most weight

**DWV rough-in.** Drain, waste, and vent is the backbone of new-construction and remodel plumbing. Say what you've run: PVC (polyvinyl chloride), ABS (acrylonitrile butadiene styrene), or cast iron, underground and above ground; venting from plans; slope, cleanouts, and test. Name the building type and the count — "[N] fixture units across [N] apartments" beats "rough-in plumbing."

**Water supply.** Copper (soldered and press), PEX (cross-linked polyethylene; expansion or crimp), and CPVC (chlorinated PVC). Name the material and the joining method you're actually fluent in.

**Fixtures and trim.** Setting and trimming out water closets, lavatories, tubs and showers, and commercial fixtures — flush valves, wall-hung carriers, ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act) accessible installs. Give a count per job.

**Water heaters.** Tank and tankless, gas and electric. Venting, expansion tanks, T&P (temperature and pressure relief) discharge, and gas connections where your license covers gas.

**Gas piping.** Only where you're licensed or endorsed for it. Black iron, CSST (corrugated stainless steel tubing), pressure testing, and appliance connections. Name the endorsement that covers it.

**Service and diagnosis.** Service plumbers should give this its own line: calls per day, the mix of jobs (leaks, stoppages, water heater replacements, repipes), camera inspection, cable and hydro-jet drain cleaning, and customer-facing work — quoting and explaining options.

**Codes and inspections.** The code you build to — IPC (International Plumbing Code, from the ICC) or UPC (Uniform Plumbing Code, from IAPMO), with state amendments — and your inspection record: rough-in, top-out, and final inspections passed, and how often you needed a re-inspection. A clean inspection record is a real, nameable strength; say it with numbers. Specialty systems — backflow assemblies, grease interceptors, medical gas — get their own line.

## Format notes

**Length follows your history, not a page rule.** Two pages is normal and expected once you have more than one substantive role to describe; one page is right for apprentices in the first year or two. Order matters more than length: license and certification block near the top in its own labeled section (screening software keys on it), then experience with a scope line atop each job.

One genuine exception worth knowing: **federal applications through USAJOBS cap at two pages** as of the September 2025 OPM change. That's a hard requirement, not a style preference.

Otherwise: reverse chronological, single column, standard headings, real text rather than graphics or tables — contractor and service-company hiring systems parse plain layouts most reliably. Skip the graphics.

## A bullet bank you can adapt — keep only what's true

- "Journeyman plumber, [state] license #[N]; [residential/commercial/multifamily] work on [N] projects over [N] years"
- "Roughed in DWV ([PVC/cast iron]) and [PEX/copper] water supply for [N] units on a [N]-story [multifamily/commercial] build to the [IPC/UPC]"
- "Set and trimmed [N] fixtures per [project/week], including [flush valves/wall-hung carriers/ADA installs]"
- "Installed [N] [tank/tankless] water heaters per [month/year], including venting, expansion tanks, and gas connections under [state] gas endorsement"
- "Ran [N] service calls per day: leak repair, drain cleaning ([cable/hydro-jet]), camera inspection, and water heater replacement"
- "Passed [N] rough-in and final inspections with [N] re-inspections across [N] projects"
- "Installed and tested [N] backflow prevention assemblies as an ASSE 5110 tester, [certifying body]"
- "Led a crew of [N] plumbers and apprentices; scheduled inspections and coordinated with [GC (general contractor)/other trades]"

Each bullet is a frame, not a claim. If a line isn't true of your work, it doesn't go on your page — an interviewer's first follow-up question will find it.

## What screening software looks for on a plumber resume

Contractor and service-company filters read for the vocabulary of the trade, and a human reads for the same words minutes later. The terms that typically matter: plumber, journeyman, master plumber, apprentice, license, DWV, rough-in, water supply, PEX, copper, CPVC, cast iron, fixtures, water heater, tankless, gas piping, backflow, ASSE 5110, medical gas, ASSE 6010, IPC, UPC, inspections, drain cleaning, camera inspection, service calls, OSHA 30, NCCER, and UA.

One honesty note on job codes: [O*NET profiles this work under Plumbers, Pipefitters, and Steamfitters (47-2152.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/47-2152.00). The three trades share one code, so your license line and the code, systems, and inspections you name have to carry what the occupation code can't. If your work overlaps other trades, our guides for [pipefitters](/guides/pipefitter-resume/), [HVAC technicians](/guides/hvac-technician-resume/), and [journeyman electricians](/guides/journeyman-electrician-resume/) cover them the same honest way.

The highest-risk lines are the ones that overstate scope — a license tier you haven't tested for, gas work written without the endorsement that covers it, or a code you've heard of but never built to. Plumbing is a small world, and the board and the contractor check.
