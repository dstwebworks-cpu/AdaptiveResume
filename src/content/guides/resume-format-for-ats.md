Somewhere along the way you were told resumes get rejected by software before a human ever sees them — and probably shown a scary statistic to prove it. Take a breath. Formatting for applicant tracking systems is real, but it's plumbing, not wizardry: the software needs to read your file and drop your jobs, dates, and skills into the right fields. Here's the resume format that parses cleanly, what genuinely breaks, and the myths you can stop worrying about.

## First, the myth to clear out

You've seen the claim that "75% of resumes are rejected by ATS before a human ever sees them." That number doesn't trace to any solid evidence — it's industry folklore, repeated because it sells resume services. We won't repeat it as fact, and you shouldn't plan around it. What an ATS actually does is **parse** your resume into fields (name, titles, employers, dates, skills, education), **store** it in a searchable database, and let recruiters **search and filter**. Humans still do the reviewing. Automatic knockouts exist, but only for explicit employer-set questions — "are you authorized to work in the US," "do you hold an active CDL" — not a robot judging your font.

So the goal of an ATS resume format isn't to trick anything. It's to make sure the parse comes out clean, so you show up correctly when a recruiter searches for what you actually are.

## The columns question, answered honestly

The blanket advice "never use columns" overshoots. Here's the specific truth:

- A **two-column skills list** — short labeled items side by side — usually parses fine. Skills are matched as individual terms, so even a jumbled read-order rarely hurts you.
- **Work history in multiple columns** is the real risk. Parsers that read straight across the page can stitch text from both columns into one garbled stream — attaching your dates to the wrong job, or splitting one bullet across two entries. Your work history is the part of the parse that matters most, so it gets the most conservative layout: one column, top to bottom.

If you want one rule instead of two: a single-column resume gives up nothing and risks nothing.

## The resume format for ATS, element by element

- **Headings:** use the standard names parsers are trained on — Summary, Experience (or Work Experience), Skills, Education, Certifications. "My Journey" is charming and unparseable as a section.
- **Fonts:** any common system font — Calibri, Arial, Georgia, Times New Roman — at 10–12pt for body text. Fonts don't break parsers; decorative text converted to graphics does.
- **File type:** .docx is the safest universal choice; a text-based PDF exported from your word processor is generally fine. A scanned or image-only PDF is the one true failure — there's no text in it to read. Quick test: if you can select and copy the text in your PDF, a parser can read it.
- **Tables and text boxes:** skip them. Some parsers read tables fine; others read them in the wrong order or not at all. Text boxes are worse — content in them is frequently invisible to the parse. There's no upside worth the variance.
- **Headers and footers:** keep your name and contact info in the document body, not the page header. Some parsers skip header/footer regions entirely, and your phone number is a bad thing to lose.
- **Dates:** give every job a start and end with month and year — "03/2021 – 06/2024" or "March 2021 – June 2024." Years alone parse, but months let the software (and the recruiter) compute your actual tenure. Keep one format throughout.
- **Graphics, icons, skill bars:** decoration parses as nothing. A five-dot "Excel rating" reads as blank space; the word "Excel" in a skills list reads as Excel.

## What the parser is actually extracting

Knowing the target makes the rules obvious. The software is trying to fill these fields: your name and contact info, each employer, each job title, each date range, your skills as searchable terms, your education and credentials. Every formatting rule above exists to protect one of those extractions. If a design choice doesn't touch them, it's probably fine; if it does — columns through your work history, contact info in a header — it's not worth the gamble.

That's the honest scope of ATS formatting: it can't make weak content strong, and clean parsing won't rank you above anyone. It just makes sure the system sees what's actually there.
