## Why this is different from asking a general AI chatbot
A general chatbot asked to "improve my resume" will draft something fluent — and routinely inserts
plausible-sounding credentials, inflated scope, and invented metrics that the job seeker must then
catch line by line. AdaptiveResume is architected in the opposite direction, and these are factual
design properties, not slogans:
- **Ask, never claim.** We propose qualifications as questions ("did you work under this
  standard? did you complete this training?"). Nothing appears on the resume until the person
  confirms it is true of them. A chatbot claims; we ask.
- **Reasoning from researched occupational definitions.** Proposals come from what the person's
  actual roles, employers, and industry imply — the standards, certifications, and required
  training that work like theirs genuinely involves (e.g., a tire-plant quality-control role
  implies IATF 16949, SPC, and lockout/tagout training). This finds the vocabulary people don't
  know to list, which generic prompting misses.
- **Guardrails that are tested, not promised.** Changes to how the product works are gated by an automated evaluation
  suite that checks outputs against known fabrication patterns (invented credentials, scope
  inflation, seniority creep) and requires every line to trace back to the person's own resume or a
  finding they confirmed — an untraceable line is treated as a fabrication-class failure.
- **The person stays the author.** Nothing goes on the resume that the job seeker didn't give us or
  confirm; disagreements are resolved by the person, not the model.
- **Checked output, shown.** We run the checks screening software cares about on the finished
  resume and show each one — the claim is demonstrated, not just stated.
The practical difference: the hours a careful person would spend prompting, fact-checking, and
de-puffing a chatbot draft are engineered into the product itself. That is what the price of a build
buys.
